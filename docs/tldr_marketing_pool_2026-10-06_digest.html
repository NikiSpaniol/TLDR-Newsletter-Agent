THE OVERSUPPLY ERA: CLASSICAL FRAMEWORKS FOR WINNING WHEN THE MAP IS FULL (14 MINUTE READ)

hook

Supply has exploded across music, apps, automobiles, and e-commerce while demand has remained flat, creating what Alex Smith calls the "K-shaped market" where every industry now faces oversupply.

Supply has exploded across music, apps, automobiles, and e-commerce while demand has remained flat, creating what Alex Smith calls the "K-shaped market" where every industry now faces oversupply. Smith argues this requires a shift from Era 2 strategies (positioning and niching, which assume gaps exist) to Era 3 strategies (category redefinition, which assumes all gaps are filled). The winning moves in oversupply markets are: collapsing to one dimension of dominance rather than spreading capability; redefining the competitive category rather than differentiating within it (like Nintendo making the Switch less powerful to own portability); focusing on demand creation rather than discovering pre-existing needs; and commanding attention through conventions-breaking claims that reshape industry conversation. Smith maps these insights onto classical frameworks—Porter's five forces, Schumpeter's creative destruction, Sun Tzu's terrain-shaping, Christensen's jobs-to-be-done, and Blue Ocean's value innovation—to argue that the era of incremental improvement is ending and category-level thinking is now the only durable advantage.

Strategic hookIdentifying which industry conventions customers take for granted and understanding which rule-breaking could unlock disproportionate customer value is a direct customer research and competitive positioning exercise that should inform strategic business case development for category redefinition plays.

Getting players to make their first purchase on a web store is the hardest challenge in D2C game monetization, with only 2-3% of players spending money in many mobile games. An A/B test found that offering additional entitlements consistently outperformed price discounts across conversion, revenue, and new customer acquisition metrics, because players perceive added value more clearly than price reductions. Direct checkout—streamlining the purchase flow from multiple handoffs down to three steps—significantly reduces friction, with repeat purchase rates reaching around 90% once the D2C habit forms. Publishers can also drive web store behavior without changing pricing through content creator programs (sharing revenue with streamers) and loyalty currencies earned only on the web store, or by offering exclusive products unavailable in-app. The key principle is that game economies have source-and-sink dynamics; any D2C incentive increases currency sources, so publishers must ensure their economy's sinks have capacity to absorb that additional sourcing. There is no universal playbook—success requires hypothesis testing, measurement, and iteration specific to each game and its player base.

Strategic hookUnderstanding the game economy's sources and sinks before designing D2C offers directly supports building a business case for web store strategy by clarifying what revenue-per-transaction level the game's mechanics can sustainably support.

WHAT AI “SYNTH PERSONAS” CAN AND CAN'T REVEAL ABOUT REAL CUSTOMERS (5 MINUTE READ)

hook

Gina Fong, a consumer anthropologist at Northwestern's Kellogg School, argues that AI-generated synthetic personas—composite characters built from customer data and claimed to mimic human responses with around 85 percent accuracy—are useful tools but should not replace traditional consumer research.

Gina Fong, a consumer anthropologist at Northwestern's Kellogg School, argues that AI-generated synthetic personas—composite characters built from customer data and claimed to mimic human responses with around 85 percent accuracy—are useful tools but should not replace traditional consumer research. Synth personas work best as preparation aids, helping researchers identify blind spots and practice interview questions before engaging real customers, such as when exploring an unfamiliar market category like DIY home improvement. However, they fail to capture the emotional nuance, individual contradictions, environmental context, and impulsive reactions that real people reveal—details that often prove most valuable for product development, like the elderly woman struggling under a desk to connect a printer or children calling a pasta alternative "slimy and gross." Fong emphasizes that strong consumer research uses both tools: synth personas to arrive at in-person interviews better prepared, but always supplemented by direct observation of people in their actual environments and contexts. The article concludes that while synth personas are gaining appeal as a faster and cheaper alternative, researchers still need to "get out and talk to real people" to understand the full, messy picture of how customers actually behave and feel.

Strategic hookMarketers should use synth personas specifically to prepare for ethnographic research and identify interview blind spots, then validate those preliminary insights through direct observation of real customers in their actual use environments to capture emotional context and behavioral nuance that inform product strategy.
