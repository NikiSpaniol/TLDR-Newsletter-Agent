SPARSE BY DESIGN (5 MINUTE READ)

Moonshot's upcoming Kimi K3 model, releasing July 27th, will be the largest open-weights model ever at 2.8 trillion parameters with a 1M token context window, but the architecturally significant detail is that it activates only 16 of 896 experts per token—under 2% of its expert weights.

Moonshot's upcoming Kimi K3 model, releasing July 27th, will be the largest open-weights model ever at 2.8 trillion parameters with a 1M token context window, but the architecturally significant detail is that it activates only 16 of 896 experts per token—under 2% of its expert weights. This represents the culmination of a consistent trend in open models: total parameters have grown 20x since Mixtral while active parameters have remained flat in a 17-49B band for 27 months, allowing labs to increase model capacity while holding per-token compute constant and shifting costs from scarce compute and bandwidth to cheaper storage. Companion architectural trends like attention compression are simultaneously shrinking the KV cache—V4-Pro's 1M-context cache is 10% the size of its predecessor's—so sparsity and compression together move the cost burden from bandwidth scarcity to capacity constraints. Sparsity enables frontier-scale inference at mid-size dense model costs but requires 10-plus H200s just to load K3's 1.4TB weights, and the serving economics diverge sharply: in low-batch enterprise self-hosting scenarios, tiered memory with hot experts in HBM and cold experts in DRAM becomes viable, but in hyperscale production environments with hundreds of concurrent requests, most experts remain hot and sparsity converts bandwidth demand into raw HBM capacity demand instead. The article argues that router design—whether routers can be trained to create deliberate locality and predictable expert paths rather than uniform distribution—will determine whether cheap-capacity DRAM tiering can scale beyond the self-hosting niche.

ONLINE LEARNING FOR COST-EFFICIENT LLM ROUTING (6 MINUTE READ)

Ramp built an internal LLM gateway that processes trillions of tokens daily and implemented dynamic routing logic to optimize across different model providers and service tiers.

Ramp built an internal LLM gateway that processes trillions of tokens daily and implemented dynamic routing logic to optimize across different model providers and service tiers. The system uses Thompson Sampling to learn real-time latency and failure distributions, tracking failure rates via exponentially-weighted moving average and modeling log-latency with a Normal-Inverse-Gamma conjugate prior stored in Redis. When routing requests, the gateway calculates the probability of a bad outcome (either failure or latency exceeding deadline) and combines this with relative model costs to select the optimal route, falling back as needed. Deployed initially to an LLM reranker use case with a six-second deadline across Gemini and GPT models at different service tiers, the system achieved over 25% cost savings with no error rate degradation, and 30% savings when extended to their streaming use case. The approach automatically handles time-of-day variations in performance (flexing away from OpenAI's default tier during business hours when it degrades) and provider-specific issues like rate limiting without manual intervention.

LANGUAGE MODEL HARNESSES ARE COMPOSITIONAL GENERALIZERS (49 MINUTE READ)

The article proposes that language models suffer from poor compositional generalization—the ability to solve novel problems by composing familiar ones—and argues that this capacity should be embedded in the "harness" (the program between the external world and the neural network) rather than relying solely on post-training.

The article proposes that language models suffer from poor compositional generalization—the ability to solve novel problems by composing familiar ones—and argues that this capacity should be embedded in the "harness" (the program between the external world and the neural network) rather than relying solely on post-training. The authors introduce the Recursive Language Model (RLM) harness, which uses context offloading and programmatic sub-agent calling to keep each individual transformer call "locally in-distribution" with training data. Testing RLMs on six benchmarks demonstrates 8–32x length generalization lift and strong cross-domain transfer: models trained exclusively on short tasks generalize to tasks 8–32x longer, and training on one domain transfers to structurally similar domains far better than vanilla transformers. The key mechanism is that RLMs induce equivalence classes over tasks with shared decomposition structure, so the root model sees token-for-token similar trajectories across problems that appear superficially different. The authors argue this approach could reduce reliance on expensive training data curation and longer rollouts by leveraging the higher-level inductive biases that harness design can provide.
