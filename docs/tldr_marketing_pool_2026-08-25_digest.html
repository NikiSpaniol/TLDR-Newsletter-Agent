THE CONDITIONAL-PROMOTION PARADOX: HOW CONDITIONAL DISCOUNTS HURT TOTAL SALES (5 MINUTE READ)

hook

A Journal of Marketing Research study by Andong Cheng and Ashley Stadler Blank demonstrates that conditional promotions—discounts requiring customers to meet specified requirements—can paradoxically reduce total sales rather than boost them.

A Journal of Marketing Research study by Andong Cheng and Ashley Stadler Blank demonstrates that conditional promotions—discounts requiring customers to meet specified requirements—can paradoxically reduce total sales rather than boost them. Across five studies using secondary data, field research, and lab experiments, the authors found that conditional promotions backfire when they combine high precondition costs (such as signing up for a two-year service plan or joining a loyalty program) with large discounts. The mechanism is twofold: the high precondition prevents many customers from qualifying, while the large discount simultaneously reduces their willingness to pay full price, causing them to abandon the purchase entirely. To avoid this "conditional-promotion paradox," marketers should minimize precondition costs, target only consumers who can easily meet requirements, and use selective communication to avoid disappointing ineligible shoppers. The study confirms that before launching any conditional promotion, firms must evaluate both the precondition cost and discount magnitude together to assess whether the promotion will encourage or discourage total sales.

Strategic hookUnderstanding the conditional-promotion paradox helps marketers conduct informed customer-journey mapping and competitive analysis by revealing when promotional mechanics actually deter purchase decisions rather than drive them, enabling more effective business case development for promotional strategies.

Dennis Wang, former VP of Accessories at lululemon who built that division from zero to over a billion dollars, shares his product development philosophy in an interview focused on how to engineer hit products. Wang's core framework is C.P.R.: understanding the Customer, engineering the Product to solve their specific friction, and generating Relevancy in the user experience. He emphasizes that successful design requires applying logical frameworks to creativity rather than relying on ego and intuition, treating product development as an engineered outcome rather than a guessing game. Wang advises that any new category launch must begin on Day One with absolute clarity on the customer persona, which then dictates vision, strategy, and execution, and that the single most valuable marketing channel is community and partnerships built around a "Muse" who embodies the brand's psychological archetype rather than transactional influencer tactics.

Strategic hookUnderstanding and defining the precise customer persona is the foundational first step before any product or strategic decision, as the persona directly dictates vision, strategy, and commercial execution—making customer research and segmentation central to business case development.

BRAND HYPE HAS EXISTED SINCE THE BRONZE AGE, SCIENTISTS DISCOVER (5 MINUTE READ)

hook

Scientists at the University of Vienna analyzed Qurayyah Painted Ware, a style of pottery produced in Bronze Age Saudi Arabia around 3,200 years ago, and concluded it represents one of the earliest examples of branding.

Scientists at the University of Vienna analyzed Qurayyah Painted Ware, a style of pottery produced in Bronze Age Saudi Arabia around 3,200 years ago, and concluded it represents one of the earliest examples of branding. The pottery was distinguished by consistent black-and-red geometric designs, stylized figures, uniform production techniques, centralized manufacturing, and deliberate export distribution that made it recognizable and desirable across communities. The researchers identified key branding elements including visual identity, material consistency, technological continuity, cultural reputation, and intentional commercial scope—establishing that brands and proto-brands existed well before modern economies. The study offers the first formal investigation into the genesis of a brand as a commodity, showing that the concept of building customer recognition and loyalty through distinctive, reliable visual identity and quality is ancient rather than a modern invention.

Strategic hookUnderstanding how ancient consumers identified and valued distinctive, consistently-produced goods can inform modern customer research into what drives brand recognition and loyalty across different market segments and time periods.
