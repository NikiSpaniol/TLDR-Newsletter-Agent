AI, TOOLS AND TRANSFORMATION (12 MINUTE READ)

hook

Benedict Evans argues that the common assumption that AI will let everyone become tool-builders and eliminate traditional software is misguided.

Benedict Evans argues that the common assumption that AI will let everyone become tool-builders and eliminate traditional software is misguided. Most people don't think like engineers or tool-builders, and they don't naturally see automation opportunities in their own work; the hard part isn't writing code but recognizing that a problem exists and designing the right solution. Companies operate across a spectrum from institutionalized systems (like SAP) to improvised solutions (like Excel and email), and as tasks scale and become critical, they must move from improvised to institutionalized—a cycle that requires organizational change, not just easier coding. Enterprise AI deployments to date show mixed adoption, with most employees using tools sporadically, a pattern Evans compares to giving everyone a PC in 1983 or a browser in 1997; real transformation happened through specific pilots and purpose-built solutions, not blanket tool distribution. Companies must ask three strategic questions when deploying transformative technology: how to buy and deploy it, what operational changes it requires, and whether it poses competitive or existential threats—questions best answered through pilots, professional services guidance, and deep business analysis, not through handing everyone Claude. Ultimately, the greatest value from AI will come not from automating existing workflows faster, but from enabling entirely new business models and capabilities that weren't previously possible.

Strategic hookOrganizations developing AI transformation strategies need to segment their workflow portfolio across the institutionalized-to-improvised spectrum and map pilot programs to specific use cases with measurable business outcomes, rather than adopting broad deployment approaches that assume universal adoption.
