MOSAICLEAKS: CAN YOUR RESEARCH AGENT KEEP A SECRET? (10 MINUTE READ)

Research agents that combine private enterprise documents with external web searches leak sensitive information through their query logs, even when individual queries appear benign—a failure mode called the mosaic effect.

Research agents that combine private enterprise documents with external web searches leak sensitive information through their query logs, even when individual queries appear benign—a failure mode called the mosaic effect. ServiceNow and Hugging Face researchers built MosaicLeaks, a benchmark of 1,001 multi-hop research chains designed to measure three levels of leakage: intent (inferring research goals), answer (responding to private questions from query logs alone), and full-information (discovering private facts without being told what to look for). Testing across models showed that simply asking agents to avoid leaking, or training them only for task performance, failed to prevent leakage; standard task training actually increased answer/full-information leakage from 34.0% to 51.7% while improving chain success. The researchers propose Privacy-Aware Deep Research (PA-DR), a reinforcement learning method using situational task rewards and a learned privacy classifier to penalize leaky queries, achieving 58.7% strict chain success while reducing answer/full-information leakage to 9.9%—lower than the untrained baseline—without agents simply searching less.

REVISITING HARD QUESTIONS WITH REPLAY BUFFERS (8 MINUTE READ)

NVIDIA researchers propose ZPPO (Zone of Proximal Policy Optimization), a reinforcement learning method that uses replay buffers to help large language models solve hard questions.

NVIDIA researchers propose ZPPO (Zone of Proximal Policy Optimization), a reinforcement learning method that uses replay buffers to help large language models solve hard questions. The approach stores questions with rollout accuracy below 50% in a buffer and revisits them repeatedly during training, graduating them once accuracy reaches 50%. This addresses a key limitation of existing methods: standard RL (GRPO) discards hard questions with near-zero success rates, while naive teacher injection breaks policy assumptions and hurts generalization. ZPPO graduates significantly more hard questions than GRPO, with the widest gains on initially unsolvable problems (0% initial accuracy). The method combines batch-constrained Q-learning (BCQ) and negative-constrained Q-learning (NCQ) effects to strengthen performance on hard examples without memorization or policy drift. Qualitative examples show the approach enables models to reason through complex visual and numerical problems with explicit, position-specific reasoning rather than generic heuristics.

AUDITING DIFFUSIONGEMMA TRANSPARENCY (9 MINUTE READ)

Researchers from Google DeepMind's interpretability team audited DiffusionGemma, a text diffusion model, to assess its transparency compared to the autoregressive Gemma model.

Researchers from Google DeepMind's interpretability team audited DiffusionGemma, a text diffusion model, to assess its transparency compared to the autoregressive Gemma model. While DiffusionGemma initially appeared to have 28.6 times higher opaque serial depth (uninterpretable computation between interpretable states), the team showed that intermediate vectors can be mapped through an interpretable token bottleneck, reducing this to just 1.1X that of Gemma. The audit distinguishes between variable transparency—understanding snapshots of computation—and algorithmic transparency—reconstructing how the model reaches outputs; DiffusionGemma achieves comparable variable transparency and monitorability to Gemma but lower algorithmic transparency due to its simultaneous token generation across a canvas. The work establishes precedent for transparency audits of new architectures with latent reasoning and identifies diffusion-specific phenomena like retroactive self-correction, token smearing, and non-chronological reasoning.

NVIDIA'S AUTONOMOUS ROBOTICS RESEARCH (6 MINUTE READ)

NVIDIA researchers have developed ENPIRE, a framework that enables AI coding agents to autonomously improve robot manipulation policies through a closed-loop physical feedback system. The system comprises four modules: an Environment module for automatic reset and verification, a Policy Improvement module for algorithm refinement, a Rollout module for parallel robot evaluation, and an Evolution module where coding agents analyze failures and improve training code. Three different coding agents (Codex, Claude Code, and Kimi Code) were tested and autonomously achieved 99% success rates on complex dexterous tasks including object insertion, pin organization, and zip-tie cutting in the real world. The researchers also introduced two efficiency metrics—Mean Robot Utilization and Mean Token Utilization—and demonstrated that the system scales across robot fleets, though with tradeoffs in resource efficiency and token consumption as fleet size increases.
