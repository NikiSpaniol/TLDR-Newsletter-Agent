WHY IS SPECULATIVE DECODING FAST? (4 MINUTE READ)

Speculative decoding accelerates language model inference not by reducing computational work, but by changing the type of work GPUs perform.

Speculative decoding accelerates language model inference not by reducing computational work, but by changing the type of work GPUs perform. The technique uses a small draft model to propose multiple tokens at once, which a larger model then verifies in parallel in a single forward pass. Traditional token-by-token decoding is memory bound—the GPU waits for parameters to load while compute capacity sits idle—whereas speculative decoding converts this to compute-bound work by processing multiple tokens simultaneously. This allows GPUs to use their "free" compute capacity at small batch sizes, completing sequences faster despite doing more total floating-point operations. However, at high batch sizes where the GPU is already compute bound, speculative decoding wastes resources by performing unnecessary verification work, which is why production systems like vLLM allow disabling it based on batch size, and why researchers are exploring dynamic speculation depths that vary by batch size.

AI SCALING VS HUMAN SCALING (12 MINUTE READ)

hook

Toby Ord examines whether AI models scale as efficiently with additional tokens as humans do with additional time, finding substantial data limitations but suggestive evidence that AI systems have historically scaled worse than humans.

Toby Ord examines whether AI models scale as efficiently with additional tokens as humans do with additional time, finding substantial data limitations but suggestive evidence that AI systems have historically scaled worse than humans. Using a UK AISI chart measuring AI performance on hacking tasks via "time horizon" (how long such work typically takes a human), Ord calculates a scaling parameter gamma for each model, finding all values below 1, meaning 10x more tokens yields less than 10x improvement—though gamma has improved over time from requiring 10,000x tokens for 10x performance improvement to just 20x as recently as May 2026. He stresses the analysis has serious limitations, including reliance on a single experiment and potential measurement uncertainty, but argues the findings matter for understanding inference scaling and recursive self-improvement scenarios, since if gamma remains below 1, intelligence explosion becomes harder to trigger than previously modeled. The work suggests AI scaling efficiency may be approaching parity with humans, or could eventually exceed it, making continued measurement of this parameter critical.

Strategic hookUnderstanding how AI systems scale with computational resources relative to human performance provides essential data for building business cases around AI deployment decisions and projecting long-term capability trajectories that drive customer value propositions.

Teams cost 1.8 to 5.1 times more than single agents, with only one statistically significant improvement: Sol's medium-effort team gained 7.3 points over its single agent (p = 0.005), while other score differences of −0.3 to +3.4 points were not significant. WHAT IS SPECULATIVE DECODING? (70 MINUTE READ)

Speculative decoding is a technique that accelerates language model inference by having a fast "drafter" model propose multiple candidate tokens, which are then verified in a single pass by the target model, keeping tokens where they agree.

Speculative decoding is a technique that accelerates language model inference by having a fast "drafter" model propose multiple candidate tokens, which are then verified in a single pass by the target model, keeping tokens where they agree. The approach works because during decoding, GPU time is dominated by memory bandwidth (reading model weights) rather than compute, so scoring multiple tokens in one forward pass is nearly free compared to scoring them separately. The article derives speculative decoding from first principles, covering key variants including greedy matching, speculative sampling with probability distributions, Gumbel coupling for reproducibility, token trees for verifying multiple branches simultaneously, and block verification which accepts token prefixes jointly rather than token-by-token. Critical engineering considerations include how verification bottlenecks emerge as batch size grows (trees become impractical at high concurrency), attention kernel limitations with tree masks, and KV cache management challenges, with practical systems often preferring chains over trees in production serving. The speedup formula shows that acceptance length and drafter cost are the main levers: in a typical scenario with 40% acceptance rate and drafter cost at one-fifth of a target pass, the speedup is approximately 3.3x.

Terence Tao argues that mathematics is entering an era of "proof abundance" as AI becomes capable of solving previously difficult open problems, a shift from the historical "Math 1.0" era of proof scarcity. This transformation is possible because mathematical proofs possess three critical properties: objective verifiability, digitizability, and access to large high-quality datasets—making them uniquely amenable to machine learning approaches. However, Tao warns that blindly optimizing for problem-solving alone is now counterproductive to mathematics' long-term health, since open problems historically served as "lighthouses" guiding exploration rather than mere destinations. Instead, "Math 2.0" should redirect AI toward strengthening human understanding: clarifying opaque proofs, synthesizing research networks, building formal libraries, and exploring theorem neighborhoods—with imagination rather than technology being the primary bottleneck. He illustrates the risks through a cancer-cure thought experiment showing how objectively verifiable AI solutions could mask misalignment and erode the human expertise necessary for safe real-world applications.
