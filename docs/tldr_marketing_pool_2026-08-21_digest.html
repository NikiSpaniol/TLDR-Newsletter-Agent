HOW AI TEXT WATERMARKING WORKS (5 MINUTE READ)

AI text watermarking embeds invisible marks into language model outputs by subtly biasing word choices at each position where multiple options would read naturally.

AI text watermarking embeds invisible marks into language model outputs by subtly biasing word choices at each position where multiple options would read naturally. A secret key colors candidate words as "green" or "red" and gently tilts the dice toward green, creating a statistical pattern undetectable to readers but identifiable to key-holders through simple counting. Google has used this approach since 2024, and Anthropic began watermarking new Claude models as of August 2026. The mark survives light editing and paraphrasing but erases when text is substantially rewritten from scratch, since the watermark lives in runs of exact wording matches, not in meaning. Detection requires the provider's secret key and is probabilistic rather than deterministic—a found watermark indicates "processed by" an AI model, not necessarily "written by" one, and absence of a watermark proves little about actual authorship.

Nathan Baugh's essay introduces the concept of "Narrative Debt"—the invisible obligation a storyteller creates when raising questions and making promises to readers. The principle holds that effective narratives always owe the audience something, creating suspense that keeps them engaged. Baugh illustrates the concept through Steve Jobs' 2007 iPhone launch and the opening of Suzanne Collins' The Hunger Games, demonstrating how different storytellers manage debt differently: Jobs creates one large debt upfront and pays it gradually, while Collins cascades smaller debts into larger ones. The article argues that the best stories maintain focused narrative debt portfolios with three tiers—major questions (mortgage-level, open until the finale), subplot threads (car loans, spanning multiple scenes), and scene-level questions (coffee purchases, opening and closing within scenes). Baugh emphasizes that stories lacking debt give readers no reason to continue, while too much scattered debt causes narrative sprawl, and that successful storytellers like J.K. Rowling track only a few major plot threads to maintain focus.
