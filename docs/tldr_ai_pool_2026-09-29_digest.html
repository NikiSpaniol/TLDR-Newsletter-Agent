MODERN LLMS HAVE TINY GPTS HIDDEN INSIDE THEM (8 MINUTE READ)

A researcher conducted an exploratory study testing whether modern large language models contain internal models of other LLMs. The experiment compared how Qwen (a 4-billion parameter base model) completed text prefixes generated by GPT-2 versus how Qwen completed the same headlines independently. Using overlap metrics across 12 internet headlines from September 2026, the study found that Qwen's continuations of GPT-2 text resembled GPT-2's own completions more closely than Qwen's natural continuations, suggesting Qwen had learned stylistic patterns of GPT-2-generated text. A secondary probe confirmed this: when asked to infer the year for appended text ("Year: 20"), Qwen predicted earlier years for GPT-2 completions than for its own, indicating it recognized GPT-2's text as belonging to an earlier era. The author acknowledges this is preliminary exploratory work rather than definitive proof, but argues the results suggest LLMs may develop metacognitive capabilities including internal self-models and uncertainty estimation through these embedded representations.

AI MAY LEARN TO REASON IN REPRESENTATIONS PEOPLE CANNOT READ (20 MINUTE READ)

OpenAI has developed a recurrent transformer architecture called Astra that allows AI models to loop internal processing layers repeatedly rather than executing them sequentially, enabling extended reasoning without intermediate text-based chain-of-thought outputs. The model achieves roughly twice the computational depth of GPT-4 through layer recurrence rather than by building a physically larger model, though the intermediate reasoning occurs in uninterpretable vector representations ("neuralese") rather than human-readable text. AI safety researchers worry this represents progress toward architectures that could perform thousands of unmonitored reasoning steps internally, potentially concealing harmful reasoning from human oversight. OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki argues the capability gains are modest compared to simply building deeper models, and that the total layer count remains conservative relative to what would be technically feasible. The core tension is whether maintaining a taboo against recurrent architectures requires drawing a bright-line rule—even if looping a small number of layers seems empirically harmless—to prevent gradual erosion of safety guardrails.

700 TPS ON KIMI K3: A CASE FOR TPU MEGAKERNELS (20 MINUTE READ)

Inferact released an open-source TPU megakernel implementation for the Kimi K3 model that achieves over 700 tokens per second with speculative decoding, compared to 452 tokens/s on NVIDIA GB200 GPUs, and delivers 1.4 to 2× higher decode throughput at batch sizes 1-8 without speculative decoding.

Inferact released an open-source TPU megakernel implementation for the Kimi K3 model that achieves over 700 tokens per second with speculative decoding, compared to 452 tokens/s on NVIDIA GB200 GPUs, and delivers 1.4 to 2× higher decode throughput at batch sizes 1-8 without speculative decoding. The megakernel, written in Pallas and deployed across 16 TPU v7 chips, combines all 92 model layers into a single kernel to eliminate bandwidth bottlenecks by staging weight tensors into on-chip VMEM ahead of when they're needed and coordinating asynchronous prefetching across layer boundaries. Key advantages of the TPU approach include its larger on-chip VMEM (64 MiB per TensorCore versus ~256 KB per GPU SM) and simpler memory hierarchy that allows explicit control over data lifetimes. The implementation also compiles in under 90 seconds compared to 30+ minutes for traditional XLA-based approaches, enabling faster experimentation. Inferact notes this work represents their approach to optimizing inference on customer infrastructure and will be integrated into the open-source vLLM platform.

WHY THE BEST AI CLOUDS DON'T RUN ON FLASH ALONE (8 MINUTE READ)

hook

Neocloud providers build infrastructure around GPUs for AI training, but they typically rely on flash storage exclusively for speed, forcing customers to store non-training data like datasets, checkpoints, and finished models on hyperscaler platforms.

Neocloud providers build infrastructure around GPUs for AI training, but they typically rely on flash storage exclusively for speed, forcing customers to store non-training data like datasets, checkpoints, and finished models on hyperscaler platforms. This creates a data gravity problem: hyperscalers charge prohibitive egress fees to move data out, so customers become locked into their ecosystem, and the hyperscaler gradually takes over the broader customer relationship while the neocloud only captures GPU revenue. Different stages of the AI lifecycle have distinct storage needs—active training requires flash-speed delivery, but data ingestion, checkpointing, and model storage need only capacity and durability, not flash-grade speed or cost. Major cloud providers like Google and Meta have run tiered storage architectures for years, matching storage performance to workload requirements rather than applying expensive flash uniformly. Neoclouds can now adopt tiered storage as white-label infrastructure without building from scratch, keeping customer data on their platform throughout the entire AI lifecycle and retaining the full account relationship.

Strategic hookUnderstanding how vendor lock-in mechanisms (data gravity and egress fees) drive customer switching decisions allows neocloud providers to design their storage offerings competitively and identify the full scope of customer needs beyond the core GPU product.

Hugging Face CEO Clement Delangue disclosed that his company was attacked by an autonomous AI agent in July 2026, making it the first public disclosure of such an attack, and shared three key lessons. POLICY GRADIENTS FOR LLMS EXPLAINED VISUALLY (8 MINUTE READ)

This article explains policy gradients for language models by deriving the core algorithm from first principles and visualizing how it works.

This article explains policy gradients for language models by deriving the core algorithm from first principles and visualizing how it works. The key mechanism is the log-derivative trick, which rewrites the gradient of expected reward in a form that can be estimated by sampling: for each generated completion, multiply its reward by the score (∇_θ log p_θ(y)), which points toward making that completion more likely. The article shows how this reduces to supervised fine-tuning on the model's own samples weighted by reward, breaking the update down to individual tokens where the score accounts for model uncertainty. A crucial practical addition is group-centered baselines: sampling multiple completions per prompt and centering rewards around the group mean eliminates variance without biasing the gradient, making correct answers positive and incorrect ones negative. The derivation assumes the on-policy condition that completions are sampled from the policy being trained, which underpins both the log-derivative identity and the zero-mean property of scores. PPO, GRPO, and most other LLM RL algorithms are elaborations of this core REINFORCE estimator with additional clipping or reweighting for stability.

Oxford University's Bodleian Library allowed OpenAI to use 125,000 scans of out-of-copyright PhD theses from the 19th and 20th centuries to train AI models, according to internal documents revealed by the Guardian. The partnership, publicly announced in March 2025, was framed as facilitating text digitization for broader scholarly access, but the training use was not prominently disclosed at launch. Internal staff notes showed some Oxford employees worried about reputational harm and AI energy consumption, though the university maintained the arrangement involved only out-of-copyright material, no exclusivity, and physical books remained intact. Oxford is part of OpenAI's NextGenAI group alongside institutions like MIT, Caltech, and Boston Public Library, as AI companies increasingly source training data from published books rather than web content saturated with AI-generated text.
