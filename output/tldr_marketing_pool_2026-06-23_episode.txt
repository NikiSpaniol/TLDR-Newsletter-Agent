First up, and this is a big one for anyone thinking about channel strategy: Scrunch just published an analysis of millions of search events from February through May, tracking what happens after someone chats with ChatGPT, Gemini, or Claude about a brand. Turns out when an AI platform actively recommends a brand, people become 182% more likely to Google it, 117% more likely to visit the website, and 185% more likely to view the product on a retailer page — all within a week. ChatGPT drove way more of this than Gemini, and being named first or called "the best" in an AI answer roughly quadrupled the downstream search lift. The kicker is that none of this shows up in a normal attribution dashboard — it looks like organic search out of nowhere. For your world, this is basically a new, invisible top of the customer journey. If you're building a business case around channel investment, this is a data point for testing the assumption that AI visibility deserves its own line item, separate from traditional SEO, because right now most companies are almost certainly under-crediting it.

Sticking with the journey theme — Reddit and WPP Media just dropped some really useful research on what they're calling the "validation layer." The core finding: modern buyers are overwhelmed and skeptical, so before they commit to a purchase, they go looking for other humans to confirm they're making the right call. One in three Redditors does this kind of pre-purchase research, seventy-seven percent check multiple communities, and nearly two-thirds say they feel more confident in decisions made after a Reddit deep-dive than on other platforms. People also trust brands more when they respond directly to questions, and problem-solving content outperforms polish. This maps beautifully onto customer journey work — it's essentially naming and quantifying the messy middle of the funnel that a lot of journey maps gloss over as just "consideration." If you're mapping a journey for a client, this is a nudge to build in a distinct validation stage, and to think about what content or community presence actually earns trust there, rather than just pushing awareness content harder.

Next, something for anyone building a business case around marketing spend: a solid piece on what actually makes a Marketing Mix Model — that's MMM, the statistical technique for figuring out what your marketing spend is actually driving — work or fail. The authors say the modeling technique matters far less than the groundwork: you need at least eighteen months of consistent weekly data, real week-to-week budget variation, and paid media doing a meaningful share of the top-of-funnel work. But the bit that jumps out for us is their first recommendation — get total alignment among stakeholders on what "success" even means before you pick a metric. Signups, pipeline, and revenue each tell a completely different story, and Growth, Sales, and Finance will happily each assume their own definition unless someone forces the conversation up front. That's a very familiar problem in business-case work — worth flagging early on any engagement where marketing effectiveness is being modeled, because the metric choice can quietly reshape the whole recommendation.

Quick one for the CMOs among your clients: Maven's running a free webinar called "AI Governance for CMOs: Find Risky Marketing Use Cases Fast." If you've got a client nervous about where AI has quietly crept into their marketing stack — copywriting, personalization, media buying — this is a handy, low-effort way to get a first pass at where the real exposure is before it becomes a brand-safety headline.

On the subject of AI trust — Pew Research just published its 2026 look at how Americans actually feel about AI, and the gap here is striking. Chatbot use has nearly doubled since 2024 — forty-nine percent of adults now use tools like ChatGPT, Gemini, or Copilot, with ChatGPT way out in front. But despite that adoption, forty percent of Americans think AI will be bad for society over the next twenty years, only sixteen percent think it'll be good, and sixty-three percent say it's all moving too fast. Interestingly, it's younger adults — the heaviest users — who are the most pessimistic. So you've got rising usage and rising anxiety at the same time. For anyone developing a customer-facing AI product or feature, that's a clear signal you can't assume adoption equals comfort. It's worth building customer research specifically around trust and anxiety drivers — privacy, job displacement, pace of change — rather than just tracking usage numbers and assuming sentiment follows.

For the account-based folks — there's a nice practical piece on using AI to prioritize account lists. The argument is that most go-to-market teams already have plenty of data — CRM, web analytics, intent signals, LinkedIn — they just don't have a structured way to act on it. Rather than building one big composite score, the recommendation is to build a "signal map": categorize what each signal actually tells you — fit, timing, budget, competitive activity — then run small, deliberate tests to see which signals genuinely predict outcomes for your specific business. This is directly useful for customer journey and account-based work — it's essentially a lightweight framework for figuring out which buying-intent signals are worth building personalized engagement around, instead of assuming more data automatically means better targeting.

A quick technical one worth knowing, even if there's no strategy angle to hang on it: researchers tested twelve AI assistants to see whether they actually render JavaScript when summarizing a webpage. Turns out all six major US assistants — ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Perplexity, Meta AI, and Copilot — don't; they only read raw HTML. The Chinese assistants and Mistral do render it. So if a client's content relies on JavaScript to display key information, it may simply be invisible to the AI tools most people actually use, unless it's pre-rendered into the HTML. Just a good one to flag to any technical SEO folks on a project.

Back to something meatier — a good piece on building "cult brands," the ones with genuinely devoted followings. Five tactics: pick a side rather than trying to please everyone, like Patagonia's "Don't Buy This Jacket" campaign; build insider rituals and language, the way SoulCycle does; let founder stories become organic lore rather than marketing copy; go narrow before you go broad, like YETI starting with hunters and anglers before scaling to nearly two billion in revenue; and position the brand as a mirror for who the customer wants to be, the way Red Bull sells human potential rather than a drink. Quick metrics note: there's a nice explainer on Marketing Efficiency Ratio, or MER — just total revenue divided by total marketing spend, giving you a blended efficiency number that sidesteps the whole attribution argument. It's a clean way to set spending guardrails, and the right target obviously depends on margin — a seventy percent margin retailer can tolerate a much lower MER than one running on twenty-five. Handy tool to have in your back pocket for a business case where a client wants a single top-line number to anchor a spend conversation.
