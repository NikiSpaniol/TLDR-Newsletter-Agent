Let's start with the story that should genuinely change how you think about funnels. New research tracked millions of search events from February to May, linking what AI platforms like ChatGPT and Gemini tell people to what those people do next online. And the numbers are wild. When an AI recommends a brand to someone who's never searched for it, that person becomes roughly 182% more likely to Google the brand, 117% more likely to visit its website, and 185% more likely to look at products on a retailer's page, all within a week. Recommendations beat mere mentions by about double at every step. And here's the kicker for positioning: being named first, framed as "best" or "top," generates 389% more follow-up searches than coming last. The catch is that none of this shows up cleanly in analytics. It gets misattributed to organic or direct traffic, so brands are quietly under-investing in a channel that's actually feeding their funnel. For your world, this is a customer journey mapping problem before it's anything else. You need to know which AI platforms your client's actual buyers are using, and whether their brand is even in the running to be named first when someone asks an AI to recommend a product in their category.

Staying on that theme, Microsoft just gave publishers a better window into this exact issue. Bing Webmaster Tools rolled out four new features globally: Intents, which classifies why someone's query triggered a citation; Topics, which clusters queries into themes the way AI systems actually organize information; Citation Share, which shows what slice of citations you're getting on a given query without exposing competitor names; and Compare, which lets you track how that share shifts over time. It's a genuinely useful tool for figuring out which content themes are earning you visibility in AI answers, and which ones are invisible. Worth flagging to any client already worried about the funnel story I just gave you.

But before anyone gets too excited and starts gaming the system, a word of caution from Lily Ray at Amsive. She's warning that a lot of the AI-search tactics working right now, self-promotional listicles, comparison pages, paid mentions dressed up as digital PR, are quietly building liabilities. Google already changed how it ranks self-promotional listicles back in January, and it hit around forty companies, one of which had two thousand of these pages live. Ray thinks a Penguin-style crackdown on paid mentions is coming, similar to the link-buying penalties from over a decade ago. Her point is that genuine expertise and consistent credibility compound, and manipulation tactics don't. If you're advising a client on their AI visibility strategy, this is worth an actual audit line item: which of their current tactics are aligned with how these platforms are designed to work, versus which ones are just growth hacks waiting to be penalized. That's a real reputational risk to price into any business case.

And one more in this AI-search cluster, purely as a technical flag: someone tested whether twelve major AI assistants actually render JavaScript when they crawl a page, or just read raw HTML. Turns out the six big Western ones, ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Perplexity, Meta AI, and Copilot, only read raw HTML and miss anything loaded dynamically. The Chinese assistants and Mistral do render it. If a client's website leans heavily on JavaScript for its content, this is genuinely useful engineering context, but I'll leave it there.

Switching gears to something that speaks directly to journey mapping. Reddit and WPP Media put out research on how people build confidence before they buy. Seventy-seven percent of Redditors check two or more communities before committing to a purchase, a third use Reddit specifically for pre-purchase research, and 63% say they trust decisions shaped by Reddit more than other platforms. The mechanism seems to be Reddit's structure itself: upvote-ranked comments, dialogue instead of polished copy, visible disclosure. Brands that get transparent about sponsorship on Reddit see meaningfully higher trust, and contextual Reddit ads have driven up to sixteen times higher purchase intent than plain keyword targeting. The real insight here is for your consideration-stage mapping. The middle of the funnel isn't a transition zone people pass through, it's where the decision actually gets made. If you're building a customer journey for a client and your consideration stage is thin on where people go to validate a choice, this is a strong argument for treating community platforms as a primary touchpoint, not an afterthought.

Now, a business case tool you'll want in your back pocket. There's a sharp piece out on what actually makes a Marketing Mix Model work, co-written by someone who's built these at Intercom, Stripe, and Atlassian. Before anyone commissions an MMM, it lays out three prerequisites: at least eighteen months of historical data, real week-to-week variation in spend across channels, and paid media making up twenty to thirty percent of top-of-funnel activity. Skip those and the model's foundation is shaky no matter how sophisticated the technique. Beyond that, it flags choices that quietly wreck models: picking a metric, signups versus pipeline versus revenue, without first agreeing who the model is for, since each metric reshapes the curves differently; segmenting self-serve and sales-led motions separately because their economics don't mix; distinguishing how fast a single ad's effect fades from how long it takes sustained investment to move the business, which people conflate constantly; and treating this as a quarterly practice, not a one-and-done build. Genuinely useful to have on hand next time you're validating whether a client's data environment can even support an MMM before they sink budget into building one.

Quick one on the data side: there's a solid framework floating around for prioritizing account lists using signals you probably already have, rather than buying more data or building an elaborate scoring model. The advice is to inventory what you've got, sort it by what it actually tells you, person fit versus timing versus intent, then run small focused experiments on one or two high-conviction signals rather than trying to weight everything into one score. If you're ever building a segmentation model for a client, that same discipline, fewer signals, more conviction, testing over assumption, transfers directly.

The pattern across Patagonia, SoulCycle, Glossier, YETI, and Red Bull is consistent: they polarize on purpose, build insider rituals, tell founder lore instead of polished copy, go narrow instead of broad, and act as a mirror for who the customer wants to be. For any customer strategy work, this is a genuinely useful lens: figure out which identity or tribe your client's brand can authentically serve, because that's the difference between paying for every conversion and getting customers who evangelize for free.
