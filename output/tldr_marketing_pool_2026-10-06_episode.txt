Morning, Niki. Here's your TLDR Marketing catch-up for Friday the 2nd and Monday the 5th of October — grab your coffee, this one's got some genuinely useful stuff in it.

Let's start with something that should make every ecommerce client's finance team sit up. Adobe just crunched a trillion site visits for its AI Traffic Trends report, and the headline is this: AI-assisted shopping might actually be solving ecommerce's oldest headache — returns. Shoppers who used AI assistants to help them buy were 69% less likely to send the item back, and 77% said the AI made them more confident in the purchase. AI-referred traffic converted 60% higher and generated over half again as much revenue per visit. The theory is simple — the AI does the boring homework, comparing specs, dimensions, compatibility, reviews, across multiple sites, before it ever recommends anything, so people end up buying the right thing the first time. For you, this is a clean audit prompt for clients: how good is your product information, really? Because if an AI assistant can't find clear specs and comparisons on your site, it's steering the customer to someone who's done that work. That's a discovery-through-retention journey question, not just a content task.

Next, a nice counterpoint to all the AI efficiency talk — brands fighting what people are calling "AI slop." As AI content floods every feed, several brands are deliberately doubling down on human fingerprints. Puresport commissioned actual painted artwork instead of AI imagery. SET Active shows the messy behind-the-scenes of product development. POV Beauty took its online community into physical Sephora stores. J Crew photographed 160 real cultural figures for its anniversary campaign. The throughline: let AI handle your backend workflows, but keep your customer-facing moments unmistakably human. Worth flagging for any brand positioning conversation — authenticity is becoming a genuine differentiation axis, not just a nice-to-have.

And speaking of AI showing up everywhere — CrafterQ is pitching itself as the fix for the classic problem of over half your website visitors leaving without doing anything. It's a trainable AI agent you drop onto your site to chat, sell, and support in real time, already used by names like Marriott and Mastercard. Worth a look if you're mapping how conversational interfaces are starting to replace the old "browse and bounce" journey.

Which brings us neatly to the big infrastructure move of the week — OpenAI launching Dots. These are always-on AI agents, running on GPT-6 Astra, that work for you continuously across ChatGPT, Slack, and Teams, connecting to over four thousand apps to do things like debug code or process invoices without being asked twice. There are guardrails — sandboxed environments, read-only research mode, approval steps for anything sensitive — but the direction is clear: agents that don't wait for a prompt, they just work in the background and learn your standards over time. OpenAI's also teaming up with Microsoft to get enterprise versions into Agent 365. If you're building a business case around any AI agent product, this is your signal to rethink the job-to-be-done — the competition isn't "which chatbot answers better," it's "whose agent do I trust to just handle it."

Now, a cautionary tale that's genuinely worth fifteen minutes of your week. An SEO professional lost control of nineteen websites in sixty-eight seconds. It started months earlier with a small, forgotten site belonging to a family friend — stolen login, a vulnerable file manager plugin — and in September, attackers used that single weak point to jump across his entire shared hosting account, planting four hundred thousand spam pages in barely over a minute. The malware was clever: it hid itself from the dashboard, faked timestamps, and quietly rewrote robots.txt to feed Google the spam instead of the real content. The fix took locking everything offline, killing the process over SSH, and rebuilding from scratch. For us, the lesson isn't really about WordPress plugins — it's about digital property inventories. Any client with a portfolio of sites, including the small, half-forgotten microsite nobody owns anymore, has a security gap that can cascade straight into the core brand experience. Worth adding "complete digital footprint audit" to any customer-experience risk assessment.

Couple of quicker platform items. Apple's iOS 27 is quietly expanding how many ad-tech and data companies it blocks from tracking — it's moved from a short public list to a private, dynamically updated blocklist that can add or remove vendors without anyone, including the vendors, being told. Pure competitor-watch territory, but if a client leans on third-party data for targeting, this is the kind of thing that can break a campaign overnight without warning.

And Reddit is shutting its RSS feeds in November and ending public API access by 2027, all while its AI licensing revenue climbs. Translation: the free lunch for social listening tools and researchers scraping Reddit data is ending — anyone wanting that data going forward pays Reddit directly.

Which, funnily enough, dovetails with a much warmer Reddit story — how brands actually build trust with subreddit moderators. You can't pay mods, so the only lever is genuine, patient value. Sonos and The Times both show the pattern: show up consistently, including the weeks you've got nothing to sell, follow the rules, support the community through hard moments, and over time moderators stop being gatekeepers and start being a feedback channel that shapes actual product decisions. For segmentation work, mods are basically a high-influence customer segment hiding in plain sight — worth identifying who they are for your client's category before you ever think about a campaign push.

Staying on trust and credibility — there's a solid IAB survey out on how creators show up in AI shopping answers. Fifty-six percent of people shopping with AI want creator or influencer reviews folded into the recommendation, and real, ordinary customers beat celebrities by a mile — thirteen percent want celebrity input versus fifty-two percent wanting everyday people with real experience. But here's the nuance: creators build the shortlist, they don't close the sale — ratings and retailer info take over at the final decision. For B2B specifically, that means identifying your genuine customer-advocates and activating them early in the research phase, before the AI model's even been consulted, rather than saving testimonials for the bottom of the funnel.

On a related note — there's some sharp new data on how brands actually get mentioned by AI search tools. A study across fifteen thousand AI answers found that third-party citations get cited constantly, but they barely move the needle on whether your brand gets mentioned. What matters is whether your own website shows up in the answer — when it does, mention rates jump as high as ninety percent; when only a third party mentions you, it can be as low as five percent. One client in the study got mentioned in half of all answers despite being almost invisible on the most-cited third-party pages. The takeaway for any AI-search strategy is blunt: stop chasing PR placements and go fix your own content so it directly answers the query. A related experiment backs this up — a Google engineer found that gaming search results barely shifts what an AI agent actually recommends, because training data matters far more than live search rankings. So if you're advising on AI visibility, the budget goes to owned content and documentation, not search manipulation.

Now, a genuinely useful piece on research methodology — what AI "synth personas" can and can't tell you. A consumer anthropologist at Kellogg makes the case that these AI-generated composite customers, built to mimic real responses with something like eighty-five percent accuracy, are a great way to prepare — they surface your blind spots before you talk to a real human. But they miss the mess: the emotional contradictions, the environment, the kid calling your new product "slimy and gross." Her advice, which I think is exactly right for how we run discovery: use synth personas to rehearse your interview, then go watch an actual person struggle with your product in their actual kitchen. Don't let the fast, cheap option quietly replace the slow, messy, essential one.

Bigger picture now — there's a meaty strategy piece worth your full attention called the Oversupply Era. The argument is that across music, apps, cars, ecommerce, basically everywhere, supply has exploded while demand has stayed flat. The old move — carve out a niche, position cleverly — assumes there's still a gap to find. The new move assumes every gap is already filled, so you have to redefine the category instead. Nintendo didn't try to out-power Sony and Microsoft — it made the Switch deliberately less powerful and owned portability instead. The piece maps this against Porter, Christensen, Blue Ocean, the greatest hits of strategy theory, and lands on one point: incremental differentiation is dying, category redefinition is the only durable edge left. Genuinely great fuel for a business case exercise — pick a client's category, list the conventions everyone just accepts, and ask which one, broken deliberately, creates disproportionate value.

On the community side, there's a good piece on event-led strategy — basically, stop planning community events around the venue and start with the question, what do you want to happen afterwards? The signal that a community's ready for an in-person event isn't excitement, it's smaller things — members already asking to meet, peer support popping up unprompted, subgroups forming organically. If those signals aren't there yet, an expensive event just reveals you built an audience, not a community. Good gut-check before any client commits budget to a flagship event.

On pricing — there's a lovely teardown of how Vitsœ, the furniture maker, justifies charging up to ten grand for a shelving system. Instead of vague "premium quality" talk, they explain the actual mechanism — no middlemen — give checkable comparisons like breaking the price into two dollars a week, and point to real resale data showing their furniture holds its value. They also refuse discounts and financing entirely, which costs them money but makes the price feel permanent and trustworthy. If you're ever building a positioning case against a cheaper competitor, this is the template: explain the operational reason behind your price, and give the customer something they can verify themselves, rather than just asserting value.

Last one — five go-to-market frameworks from revenue leaders at Salesforce, LinkedIn, Deel, Accord, and Notion. The standout for me is LinkedIn's data point: eighty-six percent of buyers say expertise matters most, but only forty-five percent think sellers actually have it — a forty-one-point trust gap. Deel's approach is fun too — rating every buyer touchpoint like a hotel review, from today's three stars up to an aspirational five. Collectively these are great prompts for mapping exactly where in the buyer journey trust breaks down, and which persona needs a completely different touch.
