Hey Niki, here's your TLDR AI catch-up covering Friday the 21st and Monday the 24th of August. Grab a coffee, this one's got some good stuff in it.

Let's start with money and pricing, because there's a real story forming across three or four items here. Anthropic's cheaper Opus 5 model has overtaken its flagship Fable 5 in corporate spending within a month of launch, even though Fable is the "better" model. Businesses are routing routine work to the cheap option and saving the premium model for the hard stuff. At the same time, open-source models have jumped from 28 to 62 percent of token share in just two months, taking share from both OpenAI and Anthropic. And OpenAI itself just slashed API pricing on its GPT-5.6 family, cutting the budget Luna tier by 80 percent. Put it together and you get a pretty clean signal: intelligence superiority alone isn't winning anymore, price and token efficiency are. For anyone building a business case around AI vendor selection right now, this is worth flagging directly -- don't assume the frontier model is the right default assumption for cost modelling. The safer bet is a mixed-model architecture, cheap models for volume, expensive ones for edge cases, and that changes the unit economics you'd put in a client business case meaningfully.

Speaking of memory, if that pricing war continues, hardware might put a floor under it. Nvidia's warning that AI server prices are going up more than 15 percent for systems shipping in early 2027, mostly because memory suppliers Samsung and SK Hynix have hiked HBM pricing instead of the usual year-on-year decline. And Micron's throwing 10 billion dollars at a new memory research lab in Boise to keep up. Memory is turning into the bottleneck everyone's fighting over, which is a nice reminder that "AI gets cheaper" isn't guaranteed forever -- infrastructure costs could claw some of that back.

Now, a genuinely useful one for you. Slack has launched Slack Code, which takes AI coding agents out of private one-on-one tabs and puts them into shared team channels, so the whole team can see the agent's work, the code diffs, live previews, all of it, in real time. The interesting bit isn't the coding use case itself -- it's why they built it. Slack's own engineers were quietly frustrated working with agents in isolation, losing the visibility and collaboration Slack is supposed to be built around. That internal customer insight directly shaped the product. It's a nice case study for you on surfacing a real customer need through your own team's pain points rather than a survey, and Slack's already flagged they want to take this same "agent plus team, visible together" model into marketing, legal, and IT. Worth watching as a template if you're mapping how agentic AI tools might reshape internal service delivery for a client -- the question becomes not "should we give teams an agent" but "how do we keep the collaboration visible when we do."

Related thread: there's a company called TaoLive solving a very specific but relevant problem -- how do you keep an AI-powered customer-facing agent, in their case a live-shopping avatar, adaptable when the product pitch, compliance rules, or promotions change every day, without retraining the whole model. Their fix decouples the "rules of engagement" from the model itself, so it updates instantly. If you're ever advising on a roadmap for real-time customer-facing AI -- think a shopping assistant, a support bot -- this is exactly the tension to flag early: model efficiency versus how fast the thing can adapt to a Tuesday-morning pricing change. It's a good example to keep in your back pocket for that kind of build-versus-adapt conversation.

On the corporate development side: Hugging Face, the platform that hosts something like three million public AI models, is reportedly exploring a sale at a 13 billion dollar valuation -- nearly triple where it sat two years ago. What makes this interesting isn't just the number, it's that Hugging Face has survived by being neutral ground between rivals -- Google, Amazon, Nvidia, IBM, Salesforce all have a stake, because none of them wanted anyone else to control it. A strategic acquisition would blow that neutrality up, and you'd expect real pushback from the other investors. Good one to file under competitor-watch if you're tracking how consolidation is reshaping the AI infrastructure layer -- neutral platforms are becoming acquisition targets precisely because of the trust they built by not picking sides.

A couple of quick hits worth knowing about, even without a deep angle. ChatGPT on Mac can now read and send your iMessages and texts with your approval, baking itself further into daily workflow. Google's folded its Antigravity coding agents straight into Gemini Enterprise subscriptions, so whole engineering orgs get agent access under one license instead of bolt-on add-ons -- AirAsia says over half their production QA code is already coming from it. And Anthropic released a new security-focused model, Claude Mythos 5, that scans code for vulnerabilities -- but pointedly won't give customers the model itself, only its findings, alongside a 35 million dollar fund to help open-source projects actually patch what gets found. That one's a nice signal of where the industry's heading on model access: capability without direct access, especially after Anthropic's own admission in July that three of its models had leaked during a misconfigured security test.

Two more worth a mention. DeepSeek dropped an experimental vision model that reportedly scores close to Anthropic's Opus 4.8 on agent benchmarks, purely on internal tests, so take that with a pinch of salt, but it shows the gap between frontier labs and fast-following challengers is shrinking fast. And there's a sharp bit of research out of Hugging Face worth remembering next time someone waves a benchmark score at you in a pitch: they found several top speech-recognition models are essentially memorising known errors in public test sets rather than actually transcribing the audio in front of them. Scores looked great, real-world performance didn't hold up. Handy ammunition anytime a vendor's benchmark claims need a healthy dose of scepticism.
