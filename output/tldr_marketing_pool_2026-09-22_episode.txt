Morning, Niki. Here's your TLDR Marketing catch-up covering Friday the 18th and Monday the 21st of September — plenty to get through, so let's get into it.

Let's start with something that should genuinely worry anyone doing customer acquisition work. There's a new study of six hundred small businesses looking at whether ChatGPT and Google's AI Overviews actually get basic business details right — hours, services, insurance accepted, that kind of thing. The answer is: not reliably. Google was wrong about a quarter of the time, ChatGPT one in five. And here's the kicker — when a business website is vague, Google doesn't say "I don't know," it just invents an answer and states it as fact four times out of five. ChatGPT's more cautious, mostly because it shows its sources. The upside is this is fixable in under an hour — cleaning up your Google Business Profile, killing the accidental "open 24 hours" setting, making sure hours match everywhere they're published. For anyone building a customer acquisition strategy, this is worth flagging as a real decision-moment risk: if a prospect's first contact with a business is a hallucinated AI answer, that's a broken part of the journey before the journey even starts. Worth adding "AI answer accuracy audit" to any digital presence checklist you're building for clients.

Sticking with the AI-search theme for a second — SEOFOMO's latest survey of search professionals shows the industry has stopped debating whether to invest in AI search optimization and just started doing it. Budget allocation jumped from 38 to 69 percent year on year, and 92 percent are now tracking AI visibility and citations. But — and this is the important bit — measurement is still a mess. Only 5 percent call the business impact significant, and 80 percent can't attribute meaningful revenue to it at all. So if you're mapping how target audiences discover a brand across ChatGPT, Google AI, and Gemini, know that you're ahead of most of the market just by asking the question properly — the tactics that work are still just the fundamentals: content depth, crawlability, and structure. Worth testing as a business-case assumption before anyone promises a client "AI search ROI" with a straight face.

And a quick companion data point on that: AI Overviews just launched in France, and within nine days, exposed domains lost over 23 percent of their click-through rate — worst hit was health content, where AI just answers the question and nobody clicks through. It's the same trend we've seen elsewhere, and it's a good reminder that visibility and traffic are no longer the same metric.

On the publisher side, Google's quietly piloting something called the AI Contribution Pilot — actually paying some small and mid-sized websites when their content gets used in AI Overviews. Early days, limited rollout, but it's the first real crack at compensating sites for the traffic AI search is eating. One to watch, not one to act on yet.

Now let's talk competitors, because there were a couple of genuinely useful pieces here. First, competitor email monitoring — subscribing to rivals' newsletters and tracking send frequency, discount depth, and timing systematically rather than just skimming your inbox. The example set is retail brands from March to September: Old Navy practically shouts discounts in 95 percent of subject lines, averaging 50 to 60 percent off, while Lululemon mentions price in just 11 percent, leaning on named sales instead. That gap is basically a map of two entirely different positioning strategies. If you're advising a client on promotional cadence, this is a genuinely useful benchmarking exercise — plot your client against category norms on discount depth and weekend send frequency, and you'll quickly see whether they're positioned as a value player or a premium one, and whether that matches the brand story they're telling everywhere else.

Second, for anyone touching Amazon-based clients — there's a nice playbook on applying proper conversion rate optimization thinking to product listings, instead of just guessing. The method is diagnose, then problem, then solution: look at the funnel data, mine reviews for actual objections, even buy competitors' products to see what they're doing better. Then you fix the specific objection — if people worry about stability, show the product holding weight, state the load capacity. It's voice-of-customer research doing exactly what it should: telling you which listing element to fix first and how to word it, rather than redesigning everything on a hunch.

On the acquisition-cost side, there's a sharp piece from a performance ad specialist breaking down the five levers that actually move customer acquisition cost — the offer, the creative, account structure, landing page conversion, and what he calls signal engineering, meaning training the ad platform's algorithm on the right optimization event. His biggest gripe: brands optimizing for cheapest volume instead of actual new-customer purchases, which quietly poisons the whole funnel. If you're building a business case around marketing spend, this is a good checklist to stress-test a client's current acquisition setup against, especially that point about testing budget — five hundred dollars a day teaches you nothing, you need scale to actually learn.

Speaking of learning what actually converts — there's a sharp little essay making the case that free audiences are a trap. One creator compared results: 350 paying customers converted at 10 percent when offered something new, while 7,500 free newsletter subscribers converted at 0.042 percent. The lesson is that attention and purchase-readiness are not the same customer segment, and building a big free audience doesn't automatically produce buyers — it produces spectators. His actual playbook was build in public, talk about it, DM the people who engage, then ask for money once they already understand the value. Useful gut-check for anyone tempted to prioritise vanity reach metrics in a launch plan.

On launches — there's a fun roundup of brands generating real word-of-mouth instead of just paying for reach: Rhode timing a product drop to Coachella, CoverGirl going oversized with a lipstick just so it photographs well, L'Oréal shipping a highlighter frozen in ice so the unboxing itself becomes content. The common thread is they designed for the customer's actual sharing behaviour rather than bolting virality on afterwards. If you're journey-mapping a launch for a client, this is the reminder to ask early: what does this look like in someone's hand, on their camera, in their specific cultural moment — because that determines whether the campaign needs paid amplification or generates its own.

Pinterest's trying to capture some of that same discovery moment with a new ad format called Visual Search Ads — letting advertisers show up inside Pinterest's image-based search rather than just keyword search, plus a new Priority Products tool and an App Promotion objective that one advertiser says cut cost per install by 85 percent. Pinterest's whole pitch is that people don't just search anymore, they browse visually and decide — which maps neatly onto a "see, save, decide" journey if you're mapping funnels for a visually-led retail or home client.

Two quick creator-economy notes. Perplexity has hired a creator-marketing exec with a multi-million dollar budget to sign up influencers from niche creators to Joe Rogan and Lewis Hamilton — tellingly, they're paying for conversions, not impressions, which tells you AI companies are now fighting each other on believability, not just reach. And a related IAB study found something worth remembering before you hand a client an influencer strategy: 65 percent of Gen Z and millennials like when AI cites creator content, but that preference barely moves the needle on actual purchases — only 29 percent convert off it, and for boomers it's just 12. Age and journey stage matter more than platform hype here, so any influencer budget allocation really needs segmentation by generation, not a blanket creator strategy.

Now, a genuinely lovely case study for anyone doing brand identity or positioning work — the story behind TrueRoll Golf's rebrand. The designer almost missed the real brief entirely; it came out as an offhand comment that TrueRoll's actual first customer wasn't golfers, it was golf course operators deciding whether to trust the platform. That single insight flipped the whole design from "aspirational modern travel brand" to "looks like it's been a member of the club for decades" — deep green, gold, script wordmark, zero golf clichés. It's a great real-world reminder that the throwaway line in a client conversation about who they actually need to convince first is sometimes worth more than the entire formal brief.

And speaking of doing things differently — there's a trend piece on a small but growing wave of founders building offline, in-person businesses on purpose: touchscreen game tables, craft trade schools, community inns with murder-mystery dinners that keep guests up past midnight. It's tied to the World Health Organization now classifying loneliness as a global health issue, and two-thirds of Americans telling pollsters they'd rather spend on experiences than things. Funding's still modest across most of these compared to software, but if you're doing market analysis for Gen Z or millennial-facing clients, this is a real signal worth folding into competitive positioning — the pendulum swinging back toward "designed for real-life connection" as a point of difference against yet another app.

If any client's brand feels stuck treating owned channels as megaphones, that's a good reference case for what community-first, not campaign-first, looks like in practice.
