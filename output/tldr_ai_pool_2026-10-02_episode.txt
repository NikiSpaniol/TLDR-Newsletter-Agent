Zooming out to workforce impact — Anthropic researchers built a robot exposure index, essentially mapping which jobs current robots can actually do versus which ones just look automatable on paper. Turns out robots can technically handle seventy-four percent of physical tasks, representing over a third of all US working hours — but mostly only in controlled environments like warehouses and factories. Cost-wise, robots are only competitive for about zero-point-three percent of actual work today, and even on aggressive price-decline assumptions that only gets to ten percent in forty years. The historical data backs it up too — jobs more exposed to robots since the seventies have already seen bigger wage and employment hits. If you're doing workforce strategy work with a client, this index is a solid, evidence-based lens for identifying which customer segments or employee populations carry real automation risk versus which ones just sound scary in a boardroom slide.

And on that note — there's a good piece out of Warp this week on the cultural side of all this. As coding shifts from engineers writing code to engineers steering fleets of AI agents — what they're calling "software factories" — the real risk isn't the technology, it's morale. Work becomes measured in token efficiency, visible by default, and the word "factory" alone makes skilled engineers feel replaceable. Warp's answer is splitting the role in two: product engineering, where humans bring taste and judgement, and factory engineering, optimising the system itself, which they've now built into performance reviews as legitimate, valued work. Any client rolling out AI-driven dev tools needs this exact conversation before attrition tells them they needed it.
