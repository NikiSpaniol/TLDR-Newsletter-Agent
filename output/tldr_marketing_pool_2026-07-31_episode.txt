Let's start with a structural shift worth keeping on your radar. For years, the "walled gardens" — Google, Meta, that crowd — have hoovered up ad spend even though the open internet actually captures two-thirds of consumer attention. The gap existed because open-web advertising was fragmented and hard to measure. That's closing fast. AppsFlyer just raised two point seven billion dollars from major platforms to build independent measurement infrastructure, and advertisers are shifting their question from "how cheap was this media" to "what business result did this actually create." That favors the open internet's performance case and quietly undermines walled gardens who've been grading their own homework. Nothing actionable for you today, just a trend to watch — the ad-tech landscape is consolidating around fewer, stronger, AI-first players.

Now, something a bit more fun: the early-2000s heartthrobs are back, and they're selling snacks. Chad Michael Murray, Devon Sawa, Adrian Grenier — brands like Pepsi's Bubly, Panera, and Vera Bradley are casting them in campaigns dripping with capri pants, poster-covered bedroom walls, and All-American Rejects needle drops. It works because millennial women now have real purchasing power, and nostalgia for that specific, hyper-detailed slice of culture creates an emotional shortcut that's incredibly shareable. The lesson for any customer strategy work is this: nostalgia isn't just a mood, it's a segmentation tool. If you're mapping a millennial customer journey, the emotional trigger point isn't the product feature — it's the formative cultural memory attached to it. Worth testing whether your own audience has an equivalent "poster on the wall" moment you could tap into.

Sticking with persuasion for a second — there's a nice piece on the "steel man" technique, which is basically the opposite of a straw man. Instead of picking the weakest version of an objection to knock down, you build the strongest possible version of your customer's disagreement, say it back to them clearly, and only then respond. Domino's did this famously in 2010, airing customers calling their pizza "cardboard" before showing the reformulated recipe — same-store sales jumped fourteen percent. But here's the catch, and it's very much your territory: this only works if you actually know the real objection. You can't invent one and knock it down, and you can't steel-man an objection you haven't validated through actual customer research. So before anyone on a client team gets excited about a steel-man campaign, the prerequisite question is: have we actually done the qualitative work to know what people are really objecting to, or are we guessing?

Okay, this next one is genuinely one of my favourites this week. Why did Spotify beat both iTunes and piracy? Not price, not catalogue size — speed. Spotify got a song to start playing in a median of two hundred sixty-five milliseconds, which is faster than the human brain can even register a delay. They did it by building a peer-to-peer architecture — over half the audio came from your own device's cache, more than a third from other listeners' computers, and less than nine percent from Spotify's own servers. They literally hired the guy who built µTorrent. And when they couldn't hit their speed target, they used a bit of psychological theatre — animating a loading spinner before the song had technically started, just to make it feel instant. The strategic insight here is bigger than the engineering trick: Spotify figured out that what people loved about piracy wasn't that it was free, it was that it was instant and total. They didn't compete with Napster on Napster's price. They competed on Napster's actual emotional promise. That's a great model for any business case you're building — before you assume the competitive axis is cost, dig into what unmet need actually drove customers to the "illegitimate" alternative in the first place.

Now let's talk about the AI-search shakeup, because there's a cluster of stories this week that all point the same direction. Google's AI Overviews have gone from appearing in fifteen percent of searches a year ago to forty-three percent now, and AI Mode visits nearly doubled in the past year. Google is quietly turning itself from a signpost into a destination — answering the question itself instead of sending you somewhere else. Zoom out further and the half-year picture backs this up: AI Mode has hit a billion monthly users, sixty-eight percent of Google searches now end with no click at all, and publishers have lost a third of their referral traffic year over year. But here's the important nuance — trust, not traditional ranking, is becoming the deciding factor in AI search. Users pick the top recommendation most of the time, but they're also willing to scroll to find a brand they already trust, and brand mentions are moving the needle more than citations do. For your customer discovery work, that's the headline: the old click-based attribution model is breaking down, and the new one runs on brand trust signals showing up inside AI answers, not just blue-link position.

Which pairs nicely with an Ahrefs study that looked at three hundred thirty-one thousand ranking pages to answer a simple question: does Google punish AI-written content? Short answer — no, it punishes bad content, and a lot of AI content happens to be bad. Fully AI-generated pages can and do rank in the top three, about five percent of them do, but pages with under half AI content still dominate eighty-two percent of top rankings, and heavily AI-written pages get indexed less and pull two to three times fewer impressions. The takeaway for content strategy conversations with clients: stop treating "did AI write this" as the risk variable, and start benchmarking on depth, originality, and accuracy — that's what's actually being rewarded or punished.

And on the more technical end, there was a great comparative piece on how DoorDash, Instacart, and Uber Eats each bolted large language models onto their search systems in three completely different ways — not because they chose different AI models, but because their existing infrastructure forced different architectures. DoorDash used AI offline to enrich a knowledge graph. Instacart split the problem between a retrieval system for common queries and a fine-tuned model for rare ones. Uber Eats rebuilt its retrieval engine around AI-generated embeddings entirely. All three kept classical systems underneath as guardrails. The strategic point for anyone advising on AI integration: your legacy tech stack isn't a constraint to work around, it's actually the thing that should be shaping which AI capability you prioritize first.

Tying a bow on the AI-search theme, there's a smart piece arguing that AI isn't replacing marketing channels, it's just crowding the room. Radio still gets sixty-one percent of Americans' audio time versus a combined thirty-six for podcasts and streaming. TV is still the top news source at thirty-seven percent even with social media at twenty-six. The point isn't that any channel is dying, it's that customers now move across more touchpoints than ever — discovery through search or social or AI chat, validation on your website, conversion somewhere else entirely. So the practical move isn't chasing every new platform, it's journey-mapping which specific role each channel plays for your customers and budgeting accordingly, rather than spreading spend thin trying to "cover" everything equally.

Switching gears — a nice bit of grounding data on influencer marketing. Five years of deal data from G&B Digital and Bobbie shows the space has actually matured rather than exploded chaotically. Average creator contract values are up ninety-five percent since 2019, deal timelines have compressed by over a third as contract language standardized, and nearly a quarter of brand-creator relationships are now repeat partnerships. If you've got a client wondering whether their influencer spend is sensible, this is a solid external benchmark to hold it against — pricing, timeline, and repeat-rate data that tells you whether you're behind or ahead of where the industry actually is.

Here's an interesting organizational one: ClickUp is hiring a single Chief Marketing Officer to run their entire marketing department — brand, campaigns, creative, social, email, content — for up to a million dollars, no resume required, just two videos. It's part of a broader restructuring after a twenty-two percent layoff, where AI agents are doing the execution and one "system manager" filters the output. This is happening at the same time some Fortune 500s are eliminating the CMO title altogether — different reaction, same underlying cause. AI has compressed how much execution work a single person needs a team for. For anyone selling B2B marketing solutions, that's a real segmentation shift: the buyer might not be a committee anymore, it might increasingly be one very empowered individual making the call alone. Worth revisiting who you're actually building your sales motion and messaging for.

On the brand side, Hilton's global CMO gave a great interview this week calling brand marketing an "irrational affinity business." His point is that loyalty isn't built by racking up points, it's built by the emotional payoff at redemption — the moment the reward actually delivers value. Hilton's leaning into creator partnerships across its twenty-eight brands and deliberately handing over some creative control to influencers for authenticity's sake. The customer strategy question this raises is a good one to steal for loyalty program work generally: are your members actually retained by the accumulation mechanic, or by the redemption experience? Those are different touchpoints to optimize, and most programs default to obsessing over the former when the emotional payoff — and the actual retention driver — sits in the latter.

Quick left turn — there's fun little study showing that products with unintentional mistakes actually sell better. Fingerprints in a product photo, a misprint, an accidental ingredient — buyers were up to twenty-seven percent more likely to purchase and willing to pay up to seventy-two percent more, because the flaw reads as unique rather than defective. But it only works for established brands and for things people buy for pleasure, not utility — nobody wants a "unique" mistake in their deodorant. The useful diagnostic here for any new product launch: is this something your customer buys for how it feels, or purely for what it does? That answer determines whether you lean into imperfection in your messaging or you quietly fix it and say nothing.

And a couple of quick hits before we wrap. Meta's family of apps — Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp — each now top three billion monthly users, though Meta just posted its first-ever quarterly user decline. YouTube's second, TikTok's a distant third at an estimated one point six billion. Good context for reach planning, though as always the real question is audience relevance, not raw scale. And microdramas — those bite-sized vertical dramas — are now an eleven-billion-dollar global category, but don't expect advertising to be the growth engine there. The economics actually run backwards from what you'd expect: ads are what push people to pay for an ad-free subscription, not a revenue stream in themselves.
