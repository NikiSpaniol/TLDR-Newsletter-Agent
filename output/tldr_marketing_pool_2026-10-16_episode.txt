Morning, Niki. Here's your TLDR Marketing catch-up for Tuesday the 13th, Wednesday the 14th, and Thursday the 15th of October. Grab a coffee, this one's got a bit of everything — trust, AI, algorithms behaving badly, and an Italian grandmother selling skincare.

Let's start with a genuinely historic little milestone. A Syracuse University study just found that YouTube has edged out broadcast and cable TV as the single most-used source of political news in America — 16.5% to 16.1%. Wafer-thin margin, but symbolically huge: first time a social platform has beaten TV head-to-head. Zoom out, though, and traditional sources still win on combined reach, and social platforms overall reach 60% of Americans versus 32% for traditional ones. The juicier number for us is trust: 61% of people now trust well-known independent creators as much as professional journalists on politics, and the single biggest trust driver is accountability — 86% say they trust a creator more if they publicly admit mistakes. Worth noting the trust splits by politics too, with Republicans leaning creator and Democrats leaning journalist. If you're ever building a content or credibility strategy, this is a segmentation exercise waiting to happen — audience trust now runs along political and platform lines, not just brand lines.

Staying on AI for a second, because there's a useful reality check out this week on the state of AI in marketing going into Q4. Over 90% of marketers say they're using AI, but the report's blunt verdict is that most of that is copy-paste from a chatbot, not actual workflow integration. The blockers are familiar: messy data, no real ROI framework, and a "more content is better" trap where teams pump out volume nobody asked for. The authors frame it as a strategy and governance problem, not a tools problem — which is exactly the kind of insight worth keeping in your back pocket next time someone asks you to build the business case for an AI rollout. The question isn't "how much can we produce," it's "do customers actually want more of this," and that's a research question before it's a tooling one.

Speaking of AI and discovery — there's a neat little tool doing the rounds called the Gemini Grounding Gap Checker. It runs your query through Google's Gemini five times, tells you which pages actually get cited, and flags the gap between what Gemini says and what your own content says. Handy, because a separate, much bigger study this week — one crunching 2,500 prompts and 136 million backlinks — found the number one predictor of whether an LLM recommends your brand is simply backlink volume and relevance, correlating at 0.57. Translation: if you want to show up in AI answers, the old-fashioned authority-building game isn't dead, it's just got a new scoreboard. Worth a look if you're mapping competitor visibility in AI search results.

And a quick footnote on that AI-traffic theme — OpenAI briefly stripped UTM parameters out of ChatGPT links this week, which was quietly nuking attribution and dumping ChatGPT traffic into everyone's "direct" bucket. Adobe caught it, OpenAI confirmed it was a bug, and it's already fixed. Blip, not a trend — but a good reminder to sanity-check your analytics whenever a platform touches how your links are built.

Now, platform politics. Meta started blocking ads that send Facebook and Instagram traffic to TikTok, across seven countries, and it's not just ByteDance's own ads — it's anyone running a campaign that points at a TikTok destination. This matters more than it sounds. It breaks the quiet assumption that platforms are neutral pipes to each other, which a lot of cross-platform creator campaigns have been built on. If you've got any client whose funnel relies on "drive awareness on Meta, convert the follow on TikTok," that bridge just got narrower, and it's worth stress-testing your campaign architecture for exactly this kind of platform-dependency risk before the next rule change lands.

Smaller platform note: Facebook's rolling out a feature called "Your Algorithm," letting users type in plain language what they want more or less of in their Feed. Nice bit of control theatre for users, and worth knowing about for anyone doing organic content planning, but no major strategic shift there.

Onto commerce, where the agentic shopping story just crossed a real threshold. Retailgentic's latest numbers show 51% of US consumers now use AI in shopping, and 11.4% of path-to-purchase volume is agent-driven — Amazon's Rufus is apparently sophisticated enough now to add to cart and auto-buy with minimal human oversight. The report's warning is blunt: this ends the "wait and see" phase. Retail checkout infrastructure was built for humans clicking through linear flows, not bursts of autonomous agent traffic. If you're building a business case for commerce infrastructure investment, 11.4% is now a credible baseline to anchor the "why now" argument.

Related and worth pairing with that: a piece this week on how AI shortlists are quietly replacing the search results page altogether. Up to 93% of users just accept whatever an AI recommends without clicking further, and ranking on that shortlist correlates more strongly with purchase than almost anything else — interestingly, things like YouTube mentions and reviews now outweigh plain old domain authority. The author lays out a five-stage loop — shortlist visibility, conversion, engagement, relationship, recall — and the punchline is that the defensible moat is the slow stuff: reviews, creator relationships, brand recall. Tools and protocols are table stakes now. That's a genuinely useful lens for prioritising where marketing investment goes — replicable tactics versus the compounding stuff competitors can't just copy overnight.

Two quick conversion nuggets while we're here. Forms.app ran an A/B test on their homepage and found their fancy AI text box — "describe what you want to build" — actually converted worse than a plain "create a form for free" button, 5.7% versus 15.1%. Turns out new visitors don't have enough product familiarity to answer open-ended questions yet; they want the lowest-effort click available. Good gut-check next time anyone proposes a clever AI-first onboarding flow instead of just a dead-simple button. And on the SaaS side, there's a solid piece on pricing pages arguing that trial users don't look at your pricing once — they check it three or four times across thirty days, each time asking a different question. The fix is consistency: same numbers everywhere, gentle upgrade nudges spread through the trial rather than dumped at the end. If you're ever auditing a client's trial funnel, that repeat-visit behaviour is worth mapping properly rather than assuming a single pricing-page view tells the whole story.

Now some brand stories with real teeth. There's a lovely piece making the case that brands are wasting money chasing Gen Z when older, wealthier, less-contested customers are sitting right there. Prima uses an Italian grandmother instead of a fitness influencer, Jones Road controls its own retail rather than chasing trend-driven department stores, Laura Geller partnered with Debi Mazar instead of a trending twenty-two-year-old. None of it looks "young," all of it looks confident. It's a nice prompt to go back to basics on segmentation — who actually has the loyalty and the wallet in your category, rather than defaulting to whoever's loudest on TikTok.

Right on cue, Saie Beauty is the proof of concept. From founder Laney Crowell's early days literally reading blog comments, to flying creator Hannah Chody to Italy to help manufacture the exact rose-gold eyeshadow she'd been hunting for since 2023, Saie's whole growth engine is "listen obsessively, then build exactly that." They're now sitting at over $100 million in revenue and second place in prestige clean beauty. If you need a client case study for why structured customer listening belongs at the center of product strategy rather than as a nice-to-have, this is it.

Also worth flagging: new research on AI influencers found that posts showing the AI character actually interacting with a real human get almost 21% more engagement and up to 17.5% higher purchase intent — but only when it's genuine interaction, not just standing near each other. If a client's weighing AI versus human influencers, this is a reason to go segment-by-segment rather than picking a lane company-wide; the psychology driving trust in each is genuinely different.

And one more consumer-behaviour nugget: a survey found 32% of US adults now prefer highlights over full episodes — 43% among Gen Z — and 61% say they'd be more likely to keep a subscription if it came bundled cheaper. For anyone in streaming or telco land, that's a tidy three-way segmentation staring you in the face: discovery-driven viewers, price-driven viewers, and parents looking for a safer alternative to social media for their kids.

Quick direct mail detour, because it's more relevant than it sounds: Lob's new report shows 84% of consumers say rising prices have changed how they shop, and two-thirds are now referring back to physical mail before buying. Trust in mail over digital is up nine points year over year. The twist is generational — Boomers want straightforward discounts, younger cohorts want life-stage relevance. Good reminder that "direct mail" isn't one audience, it's at least two very different ones.

A few quick thought-pieces worth a mention on the way out. One on Pokemon Pokopia, of all things, argues that as AI floods the market with content, "made by actual humans" becomes the premium signal — and that leaders need to decide whether efficiency gains get reinvested into more volume or into protecting time for real creative judgment. Worth a re-read if you're ever building the case for why creative capability shouldn't just get automated away. PostHog also published 38 lessons from building AI products, and the standout is that reliability beats cleverness, and the real moat is context, not interface — useful if you're mapping how a customer journey changes once "approve the agent's action" replaces "do the task yourself." And there's a sharp little essay on why OKRs keep failing and getting blamed on bad implementation rather than bad fit — the author's fix is simple: pilot any framework with one team for a quarter before rolling it out company-wide, rather than assuming one size fits all.

Last few, quickly. Gallup's big wellbeing study across 97 countries found moderate social media users — thirty minutes to two hours a day — report better wellbeing than both light and heavy users, which rather undercuts the "just log off" narrative. TikTok signed its second major microdrama deal, this time with Made By Us Studios, doubling down on vertical storytelling after its first series racked up 580 million views. And if you want something for Halloween planning, Google's launched Frightgeist, a fun little Trends-powered tool showing this year's buzziest costumes and decorations — purely for the culture, but a nice one to glance at before client Halloween campaigns go out.
