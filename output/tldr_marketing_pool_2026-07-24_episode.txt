First up, a genuinely important one for anyone building a business case around demand metrics. Branded search volume just dropped 11 percent in a month, but the auction environment barely moved — which normally would be a red flag for weakening brand demand. Turns out the culprit is AI Overviews, which now show up in nearly 96 percent of commercial searches, up from 57 percent less than a year ago. People aren't searching less because they want your brand less — they're searching less because the AI is answering the question before they get to a second search. The takeaway: branded search is quietly becoming an unreliable proxy for demand. If you're using it to justify budget cuts to brand building, you might be measuring a broken thermometer, not a cooling room. Worth flagging in any business case that leans on search volume as a health metric — pair it with unaided awareness and share-of-search instead.

Related and equally meaty: a study of over a thousand US categories and 220,000 domains looked at who actually "owns" categories in AI search. Only 15 percent of categories have a clear leader, and almost 90 percent of AI-search demand sits in categories with no dominant brand yet. That's a wide-open door. Owners with a narrow lead get knocked off fairly often, but owners with a big lead are hard to dislodge. There's also a neat wrinkle — being cited by an AI system isn't the same as being mentioned as a brand, and it's the mentions that actually drive purchase decisions. For your client work, this is a genuine research prompt: go find the commercially important categories in a client's space that currently have no clear AI-search owner, then map the comparison and buying-intent prompts where competitors get mentioned and your client doesn't. That gap is the opportunity.

Sticking with positioning — there's a sharp piece on category creation that's basically a warning against branding-as-vibes. The argument is that a real category needs a real job-to-be-done behind it — something that's literally on someone's job description. Salesforce's "CRM" has stuck since 2004 because it maps to an actual job. Gong, on the other hand, renames its category every year — "Reality Platform," now "Revenue AI Platform" — because there was never a real job underneath it, just a name. Clari's got the same problem with "Enterprise Revenue Orchestration" — too broad to be an actual job anyone owns. The lesson for any repositioning work you're doing: before you sign off on a category name in a business case, stress-test whether it maps to a genuine, ownable job-to-be-done, or whether it's just a label that'll need replacing next year.

Quick companion story in the same spirit — an article on "thoughtless features" argues most bloated products got that way because teams asked "wouldn't it be cool if" instead of studying actual workflows. The fix is boring but effective: jobs-to-be-done interviews, top-tasks surveys, designing around failure cases. If you're ever validating a feature roadmap for a client, that's your checklist — necessity and workflow fit before feature count.

Now, one for anyone weighing messaging channels. There's a solid piece comparing RCS — the richer, image-and-button-capable successor to SMS — against plain old SMS. RCS is now on 96 percent of US phones, but it costs at least double and needs data, not just cellular. The advice is pure jobs-to-be-done thinking: use RCS when the richness genuinely helps someone complete a task — comparing products, recovering a cart, choosing options — and stick with SMS for the fast, urgent stuff like OTPs and delivery alerts. If a client's asking whether to invest in RCS, that's a clean business-case test: run both channels against similar segments and measure task completion and cost per completed action, not just clicks.

Now, a meaty one on search behavior. Brainlabs crunched 1.35 million UK keywords and found short head terms are declining in volume across most categories — entertainment down 16 percent year on year — while long, specific queries are growing. AI Overviews show up two to three times more often on those longtail queries. The practical implication: ranking number one on a longtail query doesn't guarantee traffic anymore if an AI Overview sits above it. This is genuinely useful for segmentation work — you'd want to split target customers by whether they're searching short informational terms, which may be migrating to AI tools entirely, versus long high-intent queries, where getting cited inside the AI Overview is now as important as ranking.

And in the same vein — there's a sharp piece on the collapse of the traditional B2B blog. Google search traffic to publishers is down a third globally, HubSpot lost up to 80 percent of its organic traffic, and LinkedIn organic reach for company pages has dropped by more than half. The companies pulling ahead are ditching the SEO-blog playbook and building actual owned publications — original reporting, real interviews, content built for their actual audience rather than an algorithm. One example reached 1.5 million monthly uniques in three months, mostly unpaid. For strategy work, that means going back to first principles on the client's ideal customer profile — what they actually read, trust, and return to — because that's the new success metric, not raw traffic.
