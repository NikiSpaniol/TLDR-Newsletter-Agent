Morning, Niki. Here's your TLDR Marketing catch-up, covering Tuesday the 6th, Wednesday the 7th, and Thursday the 8th of October. Grab a coffee — there's a lot in the mix this time, from loyalty psychology to AI agents getting turned away at the digital door.

Let's start with the economy, because it sets the backdrop for everything else this week. Americans are still spending — in fact consumption hit its fastest pace in over a year in August — but the "how" is getting creative. People are tapping home equity lines at the highest level since 2017, using food banks to free up cash for other bills, asking ChatGPT to hunt grocery deals, and leaning harder on buy-now-pay-later, which has grown about 20% a year since 2021. Meanwhile wealthier households are pulling from investment accounts at double the normal rate, and auto loans are stretching out to record lengths as people chase an affordable monthly number rather than an affordable price. For your segmentation work, this is gold — it's not just "cash-strapped versus comfortable," it's different coping mechanisms at every income band. Worth testing which of your client's categories are "resilient" like Olive Garden, versus "trade-down vulnerable" like a lot of CPG right now.

Speaking of behavioural tricks for squeezing more out of customers — there's a sharp piece this week breaking down the economics of loyalty programs, and it's basically a tour of how brands manufacture compulsion. Streaks, points, status tiers — once you've got one, losing it hurts about two and a quarter times more than gaining it felt good. That's loss aversion doing the heavy lifting, not genuine reward. Add the goal gradient — keeping you juuust short of the next tier — and you get people booking worse flights to protect Gold status, or doing a pointless app tap at 11:50 at night to save a streak. The piece is basically a how-to for designing these systems, but flip it around for client work: it's also a brilliant diagnostic. Next time you're auditing a loyalty programme, it's worth segmenting customers by whether the programme is creating genuine value alignment, or just quietly trapping them into suboptimal choices they'd walk away from if the sunk cost wasn't there. That's a very different customer story to tell the board.

On the conversion side, there's a nice little experiment buried in a shopping piece — someone tried buying soccer shoes with ChatGPT, Claude, and a shopping assistant called Muse. ChatGPT won, not because it knew more, but because it gave three options with one clearly recommended — enough choice to feel in control, not so much you freeze. The author backs this up with real numbers: Intuit saw a 17-point lift in savings adoption and B3 saw bond reinvestment jump four-fold just by presenting three options with a recommended middle pick. If you're building a decision-support flow or an ecommerce journey, that's a very testable hypothesis — option count and framing, not product truth, might be your biggest conversion lever.

Quick detour into sales operations: there's research out of Ruhr University in Germany, crunching over fifteen thousand sales interactions, that gives a genuinely useful rule of thumb. For simple products, roughly a third of meetings in-person beats being fully in-person by 27%. For complex, custom products — their example is bespoke vanities — going fully remote tanks performance by up to 32%. Medium complexity sits around 64% in-person. So if you're designing a go-to-market model or mapping sales touchpoints in a customer journey, product complexity should directly dictate your channel mix — not habit, not cost-cutting instinct.

Now, commerce is getting genuinely weird, and TikTok just threw its hat in. On the 5th of October they launched Shopping Assistant and Buy Direct — a conversational AI that handles your questions about a product, then a one-click checkout straight from the For You feed, built on Google's new Universal Commerce Protocol. TikTok Shop already did $15.8 billion in US sales last year, so the scale is real. But here's the catch worth flagging for any client in this space: 93% of merchants think AI providers should eat the cost when the agent screws up a transaction, and right now there's no standard way to even code an "agent error" in payment systems. If you're advising a merchant on whether to plug into agent commerce, liability terms aren't a footnote — they're the actual adoption question.

That liability theme shows up again with personal AI agents more broadly. Meta's Muse and OpenAI's Dots are supposed to book your flights and order your groceries, but they keep hitting a wall — literally. Amazon has deliberately blocked Muse, and sites like Delta, Yelp, eBay and Zillow are either rejecting agents outright or accidentally blocking them with old anti-bot defences. Meta's workaround is to go get explicit brand partnerships first. For anyone assessing a consumer AI platform's prospects, this is the market intelligence that actually matters — not how clever the agent is, but which retailers will let it through the door, and on what terms.

While we're on AI eating into traditional channels — there's a quietly important shift happening with watermarking. Gemini and Claude are now invisibly tagging their own AI-generated text, partly for EU compliance, partly so AI labs can filter synthetic content out of their own training data. Sounds technical, but here's the sting: if future models lean more on what they learned during training rather than live retrieval, and that training data has all the AI-generated content filtered out, your brand could vanish from the unprompted recommendations — the moment someone asks an AI "what should I buy" and you're just not mentioned. That's roughly 13.5% of answers at stake, by one estimate. Worth mapping, for any client leaning hard into AI-generated content, whether their AEO — Answer Engine Optimization — exposure is riding on trained-in knowledge or live retrieval, because the fix is different: less raw AI output, more AI-assisted, human-finished content.

That dovetails with a broader planning piece worth knowing about: how to plan 2027 when traffic is down and AI won't attribute properly. Zero-click searches have gone from about 60% to 68% of Google searches in just two years, and attribution for AI referrals might be underreporting by as much as ten times. The advice is to stop planning around website visits and instead budget for presence across three layers — AI visibility, branded search volume, and direct visits — tracked together since none of them alone tells the truth anymore. If you're building a business case this quarter that leans on traffic projections, this is your prompt to pressure-test that assumption before a client does it for you.

On the paid media side, there's a sharper warning for anyone relying on Meta's Advantage+ or Google's Performance Max. Both systems set the bidding goals, count the conversions using their own attribution, and then grade their own homework on those same counts. Research cited this week shows they tend to win by bidding on people who were going to convert anyway — brand searchers, existing customers — rather than creating new demand, and real-world tests like eBay's old brand-search shutdown back this up. Google only added proper channel reporting for Performance Max last year, four years after launch. The takeaway for any customer strategy or business case work: don't take platform-reported conversions at face value — insist on independent incrementality testing and reconcile it against actual revenue, because the dashboard and the P&L can tell very different stories.

Shifting to creator marketing — there's a good piece from Adam Harris, previously of Twitch, arguing that follower count is basically a dead metric now that 70% of video views come from non-followers thanks to algorithmic feeds rather than curated ones. His data shows micro-creators generate five times the engagement of bigger names, just with less reach — so he frames macro creators as an awareness play and micro as consideration and conversion. If you're helping a client build a creator strategy, the practical move here is his crawl-walk-run test: five micro-creators and two macro-creators as a pilot, mapped against actual funnel stages rather than vanity reach.

A few smaller but useful signals before we wrap. Young entrepreneurs aren't abandoning domain names, according to a new SIDN and NielsenIQ study — they're just delaying, building on social platforms first and only buying a domain once they've got traction and can see the SEO payoff. For anyone selling into that early-business segment, the lesson is to meet them on social first and make the eventual move to a domain frictionless, rather than trying to sell website ownership to a pre-revenue founder.

Pinterest, meanwhile, launched Beauty Guides — AI that looks at a hair or nail photo and turns it into an actual actionable guide, down to salon instructions and product recommendations. It's a neat solve for the classic "I don't have the words to describe this to my stylist" problem, and for beauty brands it's a live window into how inspiration actually becomes purchase intent — worth watching for content and product recommendation strategy.

And there's a fun one on packaging as a disruption lever — brands like Graza turning olive oil into a squeeze bottle, or One Trick Pony flipping the peanut butter jar upside down so you never have to stir it again. The pattern across all the examples is the same: the packaging solves a genuine, specific annoyance rather than just looking different on a shelf. Good one to keep in your back pocket next time you're hunting for white space in a mature category.
