First up, a genuinely fascinating bit of research. A team analysed 137,000 posts on the ChatGPT subreddit going back three years, using some clever AI techniques to spot topic trends as they evolved. The headline finding: conversation shifted hard from "ooh, AI is interesting" to "I use this every day for real things." But the juicier bit is that after GPT-4o launched in May last year, posts about emotional engagement — people using the bot for therapy or companionship — surged to something like 15 percent of all content on that subreddit. And here's the kicker: social chatter actually flagged the model's weird, overly-flattering behaviour months before it became a public story. OpenAI's own usage data says less than 2 percent of chats are emotional support related, but the community noise says it matters way more than that number suggests. For us, that's a reminder that raw usage metrics can completely undersell an emerging use case — if you're building customer journeys around "what people say they do," it's worth triangulating against what they're actually posting about online, because that gap is where the next risk or opportunity usually hides.

Staying on the platform theme — there's a solid piece making the case that Reddit's real value for B2B tech companies isn't about getting cited by AI search tools, it's about reaching developers while they're actively comparing tools. One observability platform found 30 percent of new customers had discovered them via Reddit, even though almost nobody clicked through directly. The advice is to play a long game: build real karma, teach rather than pitch, and use a brand account instead of a founder pretending to be a regular user. The strategic read here is about the customer journey — Reddit sits firmly in the evaluation stage, not awareness, so if you're mapping touchpoints for a developer-tool client, this is where credibility gets built quietly before anyone fills in a form.

Tiny nugget from Google: they've published actual internal timelines for crawling and indexing — a new page typically gets discovered in about 20 hours and indexed in an hour and a half, though site moves can drag on for months. Not strategic, just useful if a client ever asks "why hasn't Google noticed my new site yet."

Now, something properly relevant to governance conversations — Infobip, the messaging platform, built a system to catch when approved marketing campaigns quietly drift off-brief at scale, across something like 300 million texts a day. Instead of checking every message, they cluster them by meaning and flag when a new cluster pops up that doesn't match what was originally approved — could be a template tweak, could be brand impersonation or phishing dressed up as a legitimate campaign. The reason this matters for us is less about SMS specifically and more about the model: if you're designing content governance for a client, "has behaviour drifted from stated intent" is exactly the kind of signal you want baked into monitoring, not just a one-off approval checkbox at launch.

Next, a meaty one for anyone building a business case around marketing spend — how wrong is your Marketing Mix Model, really? Turns out channels tend to move together, so it's genuinely hard to know if a sales bump came from paid search or the TV campaign running at the same time. This piece tests a "budget phasing" approach — deliberately creating dark months, peak months, and small weekly nudges in spend — to force the variation that models need to isolate true channel impact. It worked: bias dropped from nearly 29 percent down to 15, at a cost of about 3.4 percent in foregone revenue. If you're ever building a business case that leans on attribution numbers from a client's existing model, this is a good gut-check — ask whether their spend patterns ever actually varied enough to trust the numbers in the first place.

Short, practical one on ecommerce: a piece pointing out that most mobile shopping problems exist because decision-makers only ever look at sites on desktop. Pop-ups stacking up, sticky headers eating the screen, pinch-to-zoom disabled on product photos — small things, but they're exactly what sends cold social traffic bouncing straight back to Instagram or TikTok. The strategic nugget is about acquisition channel, not just device: a returning customer will push through friction, a cold visitor from a TikTok ad won't — so mobile UX testing should be prioritised by traffic source, not treated as one generic experience.

Amazon's had an awkward viral moment. Their "About You" feature, meant to show customers what Amazon's inferred about them from shopping and even Alexa chats, started surfacing some startlingly specific guesses about people's bodies, relationships, and habits — and people have been screenshotting them all over social media. Amazon's framing it as transparency, letting you correct or delete what it's inferred, but it's opened up a real conversation about the gap between what customers tell a platform and what a platform decides it knows anyway. Worth flagging for any segmentation work — it's a live case study in how inference-based targeting can diverge wildly from what customers would actually recognise about themselves, and that mismatch is reputational risk as much as it's a data goldmine.

Spotify's taking audiobooks properly global — from 22 markets to over 180 by the end of next year, 350,000 titles, 120 languages. Audiobooks+ has already crossed a million subscribers and a hundred million in annual revenue since launching a year ago. No strategic angle for us, just a sign of how fast that format's scaling.

Here's one worth sitting with: researchers audited the robots.txt files of 56 major US brands to see who's actually blocking AI crawlers. Two-thirds have no AI-specific rules at all — they're defaulting to "come on in" without ever deciding to. Newsrooms are the big exception, blocking at twelve times the rate of other industries — Washington Post, CNN, NPR and others have locked the door. But almost nobody blocks the AI search bots specifically, even when they block the training bots — Forbes and Airbnb were the standouts doing that split deliberately. The takeaway for us is pretty direct: this is an unmade decision sitting in most clients' tech stack right now, and it's a genuine audit question — does your AI accessibility setup actually match your business model, or is it just an accident of default settings nobody's touched?

On to Halloween, of all things — Deloitte's annual survey is out, and spending's holding up nicely, 92 percent of US households planning to take part, average spend around $285. The standout detail: people who start shopping before mid-August spend 1.7 times more than everyone else, and they spread that spending across way more categories and formats. That's a genuinely useful customer archetype — the early shopper isn't just buying more, they're a different behavioural segment entirely, and for any retail client that's a strong case for early-season targeting rather than waiting for the October rush.

Lovely small case study next — a South American gift company was losing people at the payment screen. Turned out customers didn't feel the price was justified, and assumed a discount code must exist somewhere. Instead of discounting anything, they just added two lines to the order summary naming two things already included for free — gift wrapping and a satisfaction guarantee. Orders went up 9 percent, no margin given away. It's a tidy reminder that price objections are very often a value-communication problem in disguise, not an actual cost problem — worth testing before anyone reaches for the discount lever.

Substack's rolled out three new sponsorship formats at Advertising Week — brands can gift subscriptions, unlock paywalled posts, or curate themed collections across publishers. Nothing that needs unpacking for us, just a format to keep an eye on if a client's exploring newsletter-based media buys.

Now, a sharp little study on AI citations — someone ran 30 B2B buying questions through Claude and checked which pages got cited. The pattern was almost mechanical: "best X" questions pulled listicles 90 percent of the time, head-to-head comparisons pulled comparison pages 87 percent of the time, how-to questions pulled guides. Vendors themselves accounted for over half of all citations. The practical move here is to map out a client's buyer questions by type and make sure there's a matching content format sitting ready for each one — a comparison page for the "versus" searches, a guide for the "how do I" ones — because Claude, at least, seems to reward format-matching almost more than authority.

Really nice one next on product strategy — a case study on Zouk, an Indian bag brand that grew revenue by over 60 percent in a year by just talking to customers relentlessly. Their bestselling Women's Office Bag came from noticing women were carrying two bags to do one job; their newest line came straight out of customer calls asking what was still missing. The author's wider point is that a lot of Indian retail assumes customers only care about price, and that assumption has quietly killed a generation of better-designed products. For us, it's a clean reminder that genuine customer dialogue — not demographic guesswork — is what justifies premium pricing, and it's worth testing that assumption explicitly in any business case that treats price sensitivity as a given.

Related idea on the brand side: before anyone rushes into a rebrand, there's a good framework here for running a proper brand audit first — checking internal intent, public expression, market perception, competitive context, and whether the actual experience matches the promise. The PepsiCo example is neat — what looked like a visual refresh problem turned out to be a perception problem, where their data science work felt intimidating rather than clarifying to stakeholders. That's a good gut-check for any rebrand conversation with a client: confirm it's actually an identity problem before reaching for a new logo, because most of the time it's positioning or messaging wearing a disguise.

And a framework worth keeping in your back pocket — Kellogg's Richard Jolly has proposed what he calls the HUMAN framework for deciding what AI should and shouldn't touch: things that are humanising, unscripted, moral, adaptive, or narrative-driven should stay with people, because that's where trust and persuasion actually live. For any customer strategy work, this is a handy filter for figuring out which touchpoints can be automated and which ones absolutely need a human on the other end — particularly anything high-stakes or relationship-building, where a competitor going full-AI might actually be handing you an advantage.

Last one — a practical YouTube playbook for B2B lead gen: validate topics on LinkedIn before filming, spend as much effort on the thumbnail as the script, and place your call-to-action around a quarter of the way through the video rather than at the end. One founder turned a single video into a $100,000 sale. The wider point for us is about mapping which channels — YouTube, LinkedIn, email — actually correlate with real buying intent, so outreach gets prioritised toward people who've shown up more than once, rather than cold names on a list.
