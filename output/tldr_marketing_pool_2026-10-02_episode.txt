First up, a genuinely useful reframe on AI search optimisation, or AEO. The argument is simple: there's no algorithm to hack here. LLMs aren't ranking pages, they're synthesising answers from training data and whatever they've crawled, so all your old SEO tricks — click-through rate gaming, backlink building — are basically dead weight. The real fix is what the author calls an LLM perception audit: compare how your company actually describes itself against what AI tools say about you when asked, and find the gap. Usually it's a mismatch between your product marketing narrative and what buyers are genuinely searching for. For you, this is basically a customer research exercise wearing a technical costume — it's worth running this audit with a client before you touch their messaging, because the gap between "how we pitch ourselves" and "how the market experiences us" is exactly the kind of insight that should shape a customer strategy brief.

Sticking with the theme of figuring out what's real online — there's a nice bit of detective work on Reddit. Someone ran the numbers on knife-enthusiast subreddits to see if brand recommendations were astroturfed. Forty-nine accounts were punching way above their weight in "what should I buy" threads. Looked suspicious. But when the researcher dug into those accounts' full histories, they turned out to be completely ordinary — years old, active everywhere, genuinely into multiple brands. Verdict: passionate fans, not paid shills, though the method can't fully rule it out. The takeaway for anyone leaning on community sentiment as competitive intelligence: concentrated enthusiasm isn't automatically fake, but it's also not proof of market share — worth triangulating before you quote a subreddit in a business case.

Speaking of measurement problems — there's a sharp piece this week on attribution for AI search. The old "search, click, convert" model is breaking because people now just ask ChatGPT or Google's AI Overviews and get an answer without ever visiting a site. A VP of Growth at Ramp makes the case that attribution has become, in his words, a crutch replacing critical thinking, and that AI-driven influence might be underattributed by a factor of ten. His fix isn't to throw out measurement, it's to triangulate — combine share of voice, self-reported discovery, and conversion data — and run proper incrementality tests with holdouts. This one's genuinely useful for your business cases: if a client's attribution model says "cut the brand spend," that might be the model's blind spot talking, not reality. Worth stress-testing any investment case that leans on last-click data alone.

Now, a nice one on monetisation strategy. Canva has just partnered with Jio, India's biggest telecom player with over half a billion subscribers, to bundle Canva Pro into phone recharge plans. Why does a company with 260 million users and three and a half billion in revenue need this? Because freemium has a ceiling — casual users get enough value for free that they never convert. So instead of asking people to pay before they try Pro features, Jio subscribers get Pro access bundled in, build the habit, and then — hopefully — stick around and pay once the free ride ends. It's a clever inversion of the usual funnel, and it's a good case study if you're ever building a business case around monetising a plateaued freemium product: the lever isn't tightening the free tier, it's finding a distribution partner who'll subsidise the trial.

Palate cleanser: Wendy's has released an actual emo album. Six tracks, real emo musicians, song titles like "My Frosty's Funeral." It sounds like a joke but it's a smart one — they've clocked that people have genuine emotional moments sitting in Wendy's parking lots at 1am, and they've turned that real behavioural insight into a cultural product instead of just another ad. No business case needed here, just a reminder that sometimes the sharpest customer insight is the weird one nobody else would bother writing down.

On the subject of understanding your lapsed customers — there's a neat trick from Couchsurfing's win-back emails. Instead of leading with a discount, they lead with identity: "here's who you are at your best," with a button that says "I'm Still That Person." The offer — a free month — is tucked at the bottom so it reads as a bonus, not the pitch. It plays on research showing that identity-framed asks, "be a voter" rather than "vote," convert better than feature-framed ones. If you're ever designing a win-back journey for a churned customer base, this is worth stealing: lead with who they were when they loved you, not with the coupon.

Bigger strategic one now: who gets paid when AI does the shopping. As assistants like Muse start making purchases on people's behalf, Amazon has blocked it while Shopify has welcomed it in. Makes sense once you look at the economics — Amazon makes sixty-nine billion dollars from advertising, so anything that bypasses the browsing and ad-clicking threatens real money. Shopify just takes software and payments fees regardless, so it's got no reason to fight it. The open question across the industry is whether AI assistants create genuinely new demand, or just reroute existing purchases through a different checkout. If you're advising a marketplace client, this is the exact question to model: segment the order book into incremental versus rerouted demand, because that split determines whether lower take-rates from AI assistants are a disaster or just a new cost of doing business.

Related note on customer experience design — Walmart's had a big response to a Pokémon card drawing system, hundreds of thousands of entries for a sixty-nine dollar box. It's the "draw" model sneaker brands have used for years, now going mainstream: random selection instead of first-come-first-served, which kills the bot problem and, more importantly, turns a transaction into an event — anticipation, sharing, storytelling. The author's point, which I think is right, is that you shouldn't measure success by conversion rate alone here. Measure whether people who didn't even win still felt like they were part of something. If you're mapping a customer journey for a retail client with any kind of scarce or collectible inventory, this is a good prompt: where's the moment you could manufacture, rather than just the moment you fulfil?

Quick one on subscription pricing: a piece comparing money-back guarantees against free trials for apps. Reading dot com swapped their free trial for a thirty-day guarantee and saw fewer sign-ups but much higher-intent ones who barely claimed refunds and stuck around longer. The logic is that asking for payment upfront filters for seriousness. If you're building a go-to-market business case and the client's paid acquisition channel is strong but retention is leaking, this is a lever worth modelling — the friction you remove at sign-up isn't free, it shows up later as churn.

Now, a useful data point for anyone mapping how customers discover brands today. IDHL analysed one and a half million AI search sessions across 175 sites and 70 brands, and the numbers are striking — AI search traffic drove a 500% jump in transactions and 312% in revenue year over year. ChatGPT still owns the lion's share of that traffic, but OpenAI's own search product grew seventeen thousand percent, and the UK's growing faster than the more mature US market. There's even seasonality showing up already — travel searches tripling around December and May. If you're building a customer journey map for any client right now, this is the evidence that AI search isn't a future-state slide anymore, it's a current discovery channel you need a line item for.

And tightly linked — ChatGPT's own ad product has quietly become a billion-dollar business in six months, onboarding more new advertisers daily than Meta, LinkedIn, Reddit, Snapchat and X combined. Software and SaaS companies are massively overrepresented, and growth-stage firms are leading adoption, not scrappy startups. Interesting footnote: companies running Reddit ads are seven times more likely to already be on ChatGPT Ads, so there's a budget-competition story brewing between those two channels worth watching if you've got clients split across both.

Now the one that should worry anyone still building a content strategy around organic traffic. HubSpot's own CMO admitted the company lost five million blog visits in a month — ironic, given HubSpot basically invented inbound marketing. The culprit is Google's AI Overviews, which now answer questions directly and keep people inside Google. Click-through rates on top-ranked pages drop 58% when an AI Overview shows up, and two-thirds of Google searches now end with no click at all. The split is telling though — transactional, product-focused sites are actually gaining traffic, while informational content, news, cooking, education, is getting hammered. The lesson for any content-led client: the old bargain of free expertise for traffic is dead for pure information plays, but product pages and comparison guides that AI can't answer without naming your brand are becoming more valuable, not less.

Couple of quick, practical conversion notes. First, a piece on ecommerce product pages making the case for removing all the mental math from a purchase — labelling bundles by occasion, pricing by the day, spelling out exactly what's in the box. Simple idea: find the unit your customer thinks in, not the unit you sell in, and price accordingly.

Second, a lovely small case study: Exclaimer rewrote one homepage button from "Start free trial" to "Try it free for 14 days" — just being more specific about an offer they already had — and got a 39% lift in sign-ups. The lesson isn't really about buttons, it's that customer research had already told them the trial length was the deciding factor in purchases. They just weren't saying it loudly enough. Good reminder to check whether your strongest selling point is actually visible anywhere on the page.

LinkedIn's testing a split feed again — a "For You" algorithmic tab and a "Network" tab showing only direct connections. Facebook tried this exact thing in 2018 and abandoned it because people just stuck with whichever feed was more engaging, which was never the chronological one. LinkedIn's also seen a million-plus reports on its new "seems like AI slop" button in two weeks, cutting flagged content's views by 40%. Read together, it's a platform trying to reward authentic voice over algorithmic noise — worth factoring into how you weight organic reach versus relationship-based distribution in any B2B content plan.

If you've got a client selling through Amazon, this changes the game from winning visibility to winning the actual explanation a shopper hears — which means you need real research into what questions people ask Alexa before they buy, not just what keywords they search.

Small thing, but a good nudge to check whether any client's seasonal journey mapping actually accounts for that weird, high-intent lull week.
