Hey Niki, here's your marketing catch-up for Friday the 24th and Monday the 27th of July — grab a coffee, this one's got some genuinely useful stuff in it.

Let's start with something that touches almost everything else we do: people are getting exhausted by social media, and it's changing how they show up online. Incogni surveyed a thousand Americans and found 55 percent are posting less than they did five years ago, over half are restricting who sees their content, and nearly half have flat-out deleted apps that stress them out. It's worse for younger users — 61 percent of Millennials, 56 percent of Gen Z. The headline isn't a mass exodus, it's a quiet retreat: people want control, not disconnection. For anyone building customer journeys around social engagement, this is a flag to test — are we designing for reach, or are we designing for the safety and control people are actually craving? That's a real unmet need sitting right there: privacy and low-pressure participation, not just louder engagement hooks.

Speaking of things quietly disappearing — so is the CMO title. Forrester's latest numbers show only 36 percent of Fortune 500 companies still use "Chief Marketing Officer," down from 49 percent just a year ago. UPS, Etsy, Walgreens, J&J, McDonald's, Uber, Lyft — all have ditched the standalone role, folding marketing into chief revenue, chief commercial, or chief growth officer positions instead. Tenure for the CMOs who remain has dropped to just 3.1 years, the shortest since 2009. If you're building a case for a client that sells into these companies, this matters a lot: your buyer isn't one brand-focused executive anymore, it's often three separate revenue-accountable execs. That changes how you pitch — lean harder into direct revenue attribution alongside brand impact, because "brand equity" alone won't clear the bar with someone whose title has "revenue" in it.

Now, a fun one for storytelling nerds. There's a solid piece on five B2B storytelling frameworks — Freytag's pyramid, Pixar's "because of that" causality trick, the "But/Therefore" rule from the South Park guys instead of boring "and then" narration. The one worth stealing is the "antagonist" idea — naming a clear enemy in the market creates tension and makes your solution feel necessary rather than nice-to-have. Worth testing next time we're sharpening a value proposition: what's the villain in this client's category, and are we actually naming it, or just describing features?

Two quick platform notes. Reddit keeps building out brand tools — Reddit Pro, Reddit Trends, all designed to help you track what your category is actually discussing before you dare post anything. And there's a companion piece on why personality wins there: Annuity.org built a single branded persona account, wrote like an actual human, and became the second-most-cited thread in its category within two months. For B2B clients selling into skeptical buyers, this is basically a live experiment in trust-building outside paid channels — worth mapping against how competitors are, or aren't, showing up authentically in these spaces.

On the AI-and-search front — YouTube's generative AI search is now pulling video answers straight into results, and creators are consistently outranking brand channels because they talk like consumers, not marketing decks. Pixability's data shows optimized channels paired with paid support drove almost three times the organic views of SEO alone. The practical move here: audit which creators already show up when people research your client's category, and look for the gap between what those creators are answering and what actual customers are asking. That gap is your partnership opportunity.

Let's do something with a bit more heart — humor. There's a great breakdown of Peter McGraw's Benign Violation Theory, basically: humor needs something a little "wrong," but safe enough to laugh at. Wiz's CISOtopia toy store prank pulled in 110,000 visitors and a 15 to 20 percent traffic bump doing exactly this. The real takeaway for us isn't "be funny," it's "find the one unspoken truth in your industry that nobody says out loud." That's differentiation gold, and it's basically free market research if you know where to look for it.

Now, an actual billion-dollar case study — Poppi. Started as a kitchen experiment with apple cider vinegar, sold to PepsiCo for $1.95 billion. The genius move wasn't the product, it was refusing to compete as "diet soda" or "kombucha" and instead inventing an entirely new category: prebiotic soda. They put roughly a fifth of their marketing budget into TikTok creators, sponsored over 9,500 posts in a year, and turned a single TikTok into 100,000 units sold in a day. This is a textbook example for any positioning work we do — sometimes the smartest competitive move isn't beating rivals on their turf, it's defining a new axis entirely so you're not compared at all.

Quick hit while we're on optimization: a low-cost airline changed its default flight sort from departure time to lowest price first. One-line change, 1.1 percent lift in bookings, worth millions annually. The lesson translates everywhere — defaults are never neutral. If a customer's already told you they're price-sensitive by choosing a budget option, and your interface still defaults to something else, you're fighting your own data. Worth a look next time we're mapping a client's journey for hidden friction like this.

And on the topic of hidden friction — wasted ad spend. TagHero's founder Brett Fish says the number one killer isn't creative or targeting, it's just bad tracking — double-counted conversions, misconfigured tags, even at big brands. Garbage in, garbage out. If we're ever building a business case around acquisition cost or ROI for a client, this is a sanity check worth doing first: is the underlying data even trustworthy before we build assumptions on top of it?

Last strategic one — Coca-Cola just rebranded, and the twist is how little actually changed. The script, the ribbon, the red and white — all kept, just sharpened. The message: consistency is an asset, and marketers get bored of their own brand identity long before customers do. Good gut-check for any client asking for a flashy visual overhaul — sometimes the smartest brand move is disciplined repetition, not reinvention.

A couple of smaller culture notes to leave you with: Substack now has an AI-detection tool that scores how much of a newsletter was written by a human versus AI, which is either brave transparency or a future headache, depending who you ask. And librarians, of all people, are hosting sold-out "Avoiding AI" workshops teaching people how to turn off Apple Intelligence and Gemini — genuinely viral engagement, more proof that "opt out and give me control" is a sentiment brands keep underestimating.
