Let's start with the big-picture piece: a survey of three hundred B2B software go-to-market leaders on what's actually working heading into 2026. A few things stood out. Founder-led sales is lasting way longer than anyone expected — companies are still leaning on the founder to close deals past ten million in annual recurring revenue. Old-school relationship channels, like intimate events and plain networking, are beating AI-driven tactics on results. And Claude has basically become the default GTM tool, especially the coding version, as teams get more technical with their AI use. The researchers describe a "barbell" dynamic: you either win through deep human relationships or through exceptional AI execution — the mushy middle is where companies are losing. For us, that's a genuinely useful axis to map competitors on. When you're building out a go-to-market section of a business case, it's worth explicitly asking which end of that barbell a client — or their rival — is actually playing on, rather than assuming "more AI" is automatically the winning move.

Quick security note before we move on: researchers at CitizenLab uncovered a new piece of spyware called AdFection, built by an Israeli firm, that infects devices through ordinary online ads with zero clicks required. It ran across four hundred and forty-two million ads on sites like the BBC and the New York Times, targeting journalists and officials at NATO and the European Commission. Google's pulled the plug on the company's ad account, but the researchers reckon this is just the tip of the iceberg. Not a client-strategy story, just a good reminder that the ad tech supply chain has some very dark corners.

Sticking with outreach, there's a nice practical piece on pitching local US newsrooms. The headline stat: local newsrooms have shrunk from forty journalists per hundred thousand residents in 2002 to under eight today, so every remaining reporter is being pitched constantly and has zero patience for vague emails. The advice is to match your pitch to the format — broadcast wants visuals and a confident on-camera guest, print wants a ready-to-run story with data already baked in — and to front-load everything so there's no back-and-forth. If you're ever scoping a PR or local-market outreach strategy for a client, this is basically a checklist for building the customer journey on the other side of the pitch: know the newsroom's size, know the right person, and answer "why now" before they have to ask.

Now, here's one I think is genuinely important for anyone building a marketing business case: a piece on "standing holdouts." Between 2017 and 2021, Uber, Chase, Procter & Gamble and Airbnb all discovered — mostly by accident — that huge chunks of their digital ad spend were doing nothing. Uber found a hundred million dollars of its hundred-and-fifty-million performance budget was dead weight. Chase cut four hundred thousand ad placements down to five thousand with zero change in outcomes. P&G pulled two hundred million in digital spend and growth didn't budge. The reason this kept slipping through the cracks is that automated bidding systems are structurally biased to claim credit — they target people who were already about to buy anyway, and then take the credit for the sale. The fix the article proposes is a permanent, rotating control group that never sees the campaign — a "standing holdout" — because it's the one thing the algorithm can't game. If you're ever building a case for reallocating marketing budget, this is exactly the kind of assumption worth stress-testing before you trust the dashboard: is this channel creating new customers, or just claiming ones who were coming anyway?

Related theme, different angle: what happens when the customer isn't even a human anymore. Two stories worth putting together here. First, AI agents — things like Amazon's "Buy for Me," Meta's Muse, OpenAI's dots — are increasingly completing purchases directly through a merchant's own checkout, using saved wallets like Stripe's Link or Google Pay. Sounds efficient, except the legal risk sits entirely with the seller. Visa's rules make the cardholder responsible for whatever the agent does, and Stripe's terms put disputes and fraud squarely on the merchant. Volume's still tiny — under a quarter of one percent of traffic — but it's growing, and it's the kind of thing you don't want to discover the hard way. Second, and bigger picture: bots are now fifty-three percent of all web traffic, and a growing slice of that is legitimate AI agents doing research or shopping on a customer's behalf. The problem is that most brands' bot defences — built over thirty years to block scrapers — now also block ChatGPT or Claude from reading their product pages, so the agent just quietly routes around them to a competitor or a marketplace. And you'd never see that loss in your analytics, because it never shows up as a bounce — it just never shows up at all. Put these two together and there's a real customer-journey exercise here: map where an AI agent would hit friction on a client's site — product discovery, comparison, checkout — because that's now a silent drop-off point that traditional funnel analytics won't catch.

Which leads nicely into another one: gated content is becoming a genuine liability for AI visibility. ChatGPT and Claude simply can't see what's behind a form or a paywall, so all those gated whitepapers and research reports that B2B marketers love are invisible to the AI tools buyers now use to shortlist vendors before they ever talk to sales. Meanwhile a competitor with the same content sitting in the open gets the citation and the credibility. The advice is to ungate anything educational — comparisons, explainers, findings — and save hard gates for genuinely exclusive stuff, like a personalised ROI calculator. Worth flagging if you're mapping a client's buyer journey: the "research" stage increasingly happens inside an AI chat window, and content strategy needs to be built for that, not just for Google.

On the consumer side, Pinterest's put out a piece arguing that brands need to stop trying to trap people in a feed and start helping them actually do something. The data point behind it: forty percent of Gen Z wish social media had never been invented, and sixty-three percent deliberately unplug — more than any other generation. Pinterest's pitch is a framework called "design the exit": open the door with inspiration, give clear next steps, then let people go live their life instead of keeping them scrolling. It's self-serving coming from Pinterest, obviously, but the underlying shift is real and worth building into customer journey work — intentional, high-intent browsing moments are becoming more valuable than passive attention, so it's worth identifying where in a client's journey customers are actually ready to act, rather than just optimising for more scroll time.

A few quicker ones now. There's a nice little piece on brand merch, using Glossier's pink pouches, Rhode's phone case, and Rivian's camp gear as examples — the common thread being that good merch makes someone feel part of something, not just advertised at. If a client's thinking about merch or community extensions, the right question isn't "what's our logo," it's "what does owning this make someone part of."

Instagram and TikTok have diverged hard on ideal video length — under three minutes wins on Instagram, three-to-ten minutes actually outperforms on TikTok. But the more useful finding buried in the data: individual creators vary wildly, one dog-grooming account tripled its views going long, a farm account lost two-thirds doing the same thing. So the real takeaway for any creator partnership is to check that specific creator's own track record before booking them, not just follow a platform-wide rule of thumb.

On email: if a sender's getting flagged for unsubscribe compliance in Gmail, it's rarely the newsletter tool's fault — it's usually that multiple systems are sending from the same domain without syncing their suppression lists, so someone unsubscribes from one and keeps getting mail from another. Worth a quick domain audit if a client's email deliverability has been sliding.

AI pricing is a mess right now — vague credits, mystery multipliers, "Maximum" plans with no defined baseline. Early adopters tolerate it because the product's useful enough, but mainstream buyers won't, and whoever makes AI pricing actually legible first is going to have a real edge. Good one to flag if you're segmenting which customer types will adopt early versus wait for clarity.

There's also a sharp little piece arguing the best marketing hire right now might come from comedy or entertainment, not marketing — pointing to Ramp's viral glass-box stunt and Meta's Muse mascot as examples of brands winning attention through culture rather than optimisation. Worth a gut-check on any client's marketing team: can they actually be interesting, or just correct?

And a cautionary one for anyone using AI to simulate customers: a founder built a tool called PlebTest to generate fake customer personas for product feedback, and it turned out the personas were systematically more optimistic than real buyers — predicting fifty-two percent purchase intent against an actual thirty-three. He killed the product in August. The lesson for any research methodology: validate it against real behaviour before you trust it, because an uncalibrated shortcut doesn't just save time, it quietly poisons every decision built on top of it.

Lastly, a practical one from Ahrefs on automating content maintenance — they built a system that refreshes data-heavy articles monthly instead of quarterly, with a human reviewing every output. Good template if a client's content team is drowning in repetitive updates rather than actual strategy work.

All worth a skim, none urgent.
