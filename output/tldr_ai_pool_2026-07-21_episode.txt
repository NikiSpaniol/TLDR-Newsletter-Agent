Morning, Niki. Let's get you sorted on AI news from Friday the 17th and Monday the 20th of July — there's a lot of model launches to wade through, so I'll keep it moving and slow down for the bits that actually matter to you.

Let's start with the open-source model arms race, because it did not slow down for the weekend. Kimi, the Chinese lab, dropped Kimi K3 — a 2.8-trillion-parameter model, the first open model at that scale, with a million-token context window and some genuinely clever architecture under the hood. It's not quite at the level of the top proprietary models, but it's close enough to matter, and it's cheap. Not to be outdone, Alibaba's Qwen announced Qwen3.8 is going open-weight too, at 2.4 trillion parameters, claiming to be second only to the very best closed models. And Z.ai — the company behind the GLM models — is on track to be the first independent Chinese AI firm to hit a billion dollars in annual sales, largely by giving its best models away free and monetizing the cloud and enterprise layer around them. That last one is genuinely worth ten minutes of your time if you're ever building a business case around AI commercialization — it directly challenges the assumption that open-sourcing your best model torches your revenue. Z.ai's model is proof you can give away the engine and still sell the fuel, the maintenance, and the garage. If a client is debating open versus closed strategy, this is your reference case.

Meanwhile Kimi K3 got so much demand in 48 hours that the company had to pause new subscriptions just to protect existing users — they're now splitting membership into two tiers, one for general use and one specifically for coding workflows. That's a neat little segmentation move if you ever need an example of allocating scarce resource by use-case rather than by customer tier.

Now, over at Google, things were bumpier. Bloomberg reported Gemini 3.5 Pro is months behind schedule, largely because it's underperforming on coding — which matters because coding is the use case enterprise customers actually pay for. Alphabet shares dropped four percent on the news. Google didn't deny it, just said testing continues with partners. Worth filing as a competitor-watch: if you're mapping the frontier-model landscape for a client, Google's coding weakness relative to OpenAI and Meta is now a live competitive axis, not just chatter.

On the product side, Google also shipped a few smaller updates. AI Mode in Search can now connect directly to apps like Instacart, Canva, and YouTube Music, so you can add groceries to a cart or build a playlist without leaving the search results page — folding transactions straight into search. And NotebookLM has been renamed Gemini Notebook, with a new ability to actually run code for data analysis, and notebooks now syncing across the Gemini app and Search. Thirty million users and counting, so if a client's using it for research work, the rename's cosmetic but the code execution piece is a real capability bump.

There was also a lot of noise around developer tooling this week — GitHub shipped a Copilot SDK so companies can embed Copilot's agent capabilities straight into their own apps, LM Studio launched a privacy-focused agent app called Bionic for running open models locally, and Nvidia released a new embedding model, Nemotron 3 Embed, aimed at cutting the token costs of retrieval-heavy AI systems. None of these need your strategic lens directly, but they're good context for how fast the tooling layer under all these models is maturing — worth knowing exists if a client asks "can we just build this ourselves."

Which brings me to the story I'd actually sit with a coffee for: Replit's "self-driving company." They've rolled out AI agents company-wide and nearly tripled code output over six months — engineers producing almost three times as much code per person, agents reviewing thirty percent of pull requests, and here's the kicker: they ripped out a seven-figure SaaS tool because their own internal agent did the job better, for a tenth of the cost. The agents aren't just writing code either — they're doing sales prospecting, marketing drafts, and support ticket triage, with hard support tickets closing sixty percent faster. This is a genuinely useful make-versus-buy case study. If you're ever building a business case for a client deciding whether to buy a specialized vendor tool or invest in building something in-house, Replit's experience is a live data point that deep integration can beat a market-leading point solution on both performance and cost. Worth keeping in your back pocket for that conversation.

On the money side, a couple of interesting plays. Fireworks, the Nvidia-backed inference cloud startup, just hit a seventeen-and-a-half-billion-dollar valuation, processing forty trillion tokens a day — comparable to Google or OpenAI — largely because finance teams are getting nervous about frontier model costs and pushing engineers toward cheaper open-source alternatives. And speaking of finance teams getting nervous: Ramp has expanded its token spend tracking product, giving finance teams one dashboard to see AI costs across OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google. They found that one in three customers already has access to a cheaper model that does the same job just as well — which tells you cost discipline in AI spend is becoming its own category of scrutiny, and probably a live conversion point in any AI business case you build: don't just assume the client needs the frontier model, model the cheaper alternative as a real line item.

Also on the infrastructure side, an inference chip startup called General Compute landed a four-hundred-million-dollar loan backed by chips rather than cash flow — the first deal of its kind — signalling investors are starting to bet seriously on cheaper inference infrastructure over pricey frontier training. And Alibaba open-sourced its AI chip software stack, explicitly aimed at loosening Nvidia's grip on developers. Small item, but another data point that the "everyone needs Nvidia" assumption is being chipped away at, literally.

A couple of heavier, less immediately actionable stories worth knowing about rather than acting on. Apple has sent legal letters to about forty former employees now at OpenAI, part of a lawsuit alleging OpenAI poached hardware talent and trade secrets for its own device ambitions — including Apple's former Chief Hardware Officer. That's one to watch if any client is nervous about IP exposure when hiring from competitors. And over at Google DeepMind, Demis Hassabis published a proposal for outside safety oversight of frontier AI labs, right as a researcher resigned in protest after DeepMind signed a defense contract that broke its own 2014 pledge against lethal autonomous weapons. It's a governance story more than a strategy one, but it's a reminder that public AI principles can bend under commercial pressure — useful context if a client ever asks you to sanity-check a vendor's stated ethics commitments against what they're actually doing.
