Morning Niki — here's your marketing catch-up covering Tuesday the 18th, Wednesday the 19th, and Thursday the 20th of August. Grab a coffee, this one's got a bit of everything: branding psychology, some brutal CRO wins, and a nine-figure valuation haircut.

Let's start with design. There's a piece making the rounds about how AI anxiety is fueling a "handmade" branding trend — hand-drawn typography, blobby imperfect lettering, scanned textures, that whole deliberately-human look. Panera's doing it, St. Regis is doing it, even Claude's own visual identity leans into it. The argument is that this started as a genuine reaction against slick AI output, but it's curdling into a formula — everyone's now faking craftsmanship the same way everyone once did skeuomorphism or grunge, until it stopped meaning anything. Worth flagging for any client leaning into this look: don't assume "handmade equals trustworthy" just because it's trending. That's a testable assumption. If every competitor in a category is now doing hand-drawn everything, the differentiation value has probably already evaporated, and actual customer research into what signals authenticity to that audience might be more useful than another blobby logo.

Two quick ones on the platform side. Google's lowered the bar for its search profile feature — creators now need 35,000 followers on YouTube, Instagram or X, or 100,000 on TikTok, down from 100k and 300k respectively. More brands and creators will now get that dedicated search real estate, header image and all. And in the "delightfully sneaky" category — Ramp and a few others, including Virgin Voyages and Indochino, are running deliberate "oops, sorry, wrong email" sequences: send a boring generic pitch, then minutes later send an apology with the "real" offer. It works because people can't resist opening a correction email. Just don't overdo it — Epidemic Sound ran this so often that people cottoned on and started rolling their eyes instead of clicking.

Now, two really nice CRO stories that are basically the same lesson told twice. First, an equity release company found their landing page was leading with a calculator — "instantly calculate how much you could release" — when research showed the real hesitation wasn't the maths, it was "is this even right for me?" They swapped the headline for that exact question, added a photo and intro from a real, empathetic team member, and broke the calculator into a conversational, one-question-at-a-time flow. Leads went up 79 percent. The takeaway for any journey mapping you're doing: the thing customers actually trust about a business — in this case, a genuinely empathetic phone team — is often invisible on the page where the decision actually happens. Find that gap between what customers value and what's visible at the point of friction, and you've found your test.

Second one, similar spirit, different lever. A gift company had upsells — balloons, chocolates, that sort of thing — buried at the bottom of a crowded product page, where nobody noticed them despite good satisfaction scores from people who did buy them. They pulled the upsell out into its own interstitial step after the main purchase decision. Order value went up 28 percent, with zero hit to conversion. The lesson: adding a step to a funnel isn't automatically friction — sometimes it removes cognitive load by giving a decision its own moment instead of making people juggle two decisions at once. Good one to keep in your back pocket next time someone says "fewer steps always wins."

On the content and search front — there's a good breakdown of what Google's leaked documentation calls "content effort," an LLM-scored ranking signal that has nothing to do with word count or time spent, and everything to do with visible evidence of real human work: original data, first-party testing, unique photography, an actual point of view. Low-effort, easily-replicated commodity content is getting quietly buried. If you're advising on a content strategy, this is a genuinely useful checklist to map existing customer-facing content against — which pieces would read as "effort" to an algorithm evaluating originality and skill, and which are filler that's about to get punished. Ahrefs also published a big roundup of 37 real AI use cases across SEO and content marketing, from FAQ generation to citation tracking in ChatGPT answers — worth a skim if you want concrete workflow ideas rather than theory.

Speaking of ChatGPT — here's a properly important one for anyone thinking about go-to-market or positioning. Turns out when people ask ChatGPT for "best software" in a category like enterprise AI agents, it doesn't run a neutral search first — it already has a pre-seeded shortlist of incumbents like Microsoft, Salesforce, Google and IBM baked into its very first query. Being in that seed list is apparently worth something like thirty-three times the visibility of just ranking well in search. Which means classic SEO tactics won't get a challenger brand into that shortlist — you need owned content, analyst coverage, and third-party validation that puts you in the same category conversation as the incumbents well before someone asks the question. If you're mapping competitive positioning for a client trying to break into an established category, this is the new axis to plan around: not "how do we rank," but "how do we get pre-seeded."

And on the transparency side, the IAB has released version two of its AI Transparency and Disclosure Standards, trying to pin down exactly when AI involvement in advertising needs to be disclosed to consumers. If you're doing any customer trust work, it's worth mapping client marketing processes — targeting, content generation, personalization — against this to see where disclosure obligations might be quietly missing.

Onto platforms behaving strangely. TikTok's piping creator content into Disney+, Snapchat's pitching AR lenses as essential rather than gimmicky, and Pinterest's building shoppable lifestyle shows for Roku. The honest read here is that these are platforms manufacturing growth stories for their own stakeholders more than genuine innovation — treat unproven interactive formats with healthy skepticism and demand real attribution before recommending spend there.

Better story on the social side: BetterHelp. Their team stumbled into a strategy almost by accident after a comment on a viral Pitch Perfect TikTok moment pulled in 300,000 likes. Since then they've built a deliberate, repeatable operation — commenting on 200 to 350 posts a month, guided by simple filters like "would your therapist actually weigh in on this?" Daily new followers went from about 4 to 450. It's a lovely example of finding an underexploited channel by watching where your actual customers already hang out and what they already care about, then operationalizing it rather than treating it as a one-off viral fluke. Good model for any client whose social presence is all scheduled posts and no listening.

Reddit, meanwhile, is testing native audio and video versions of popular posts, because creators keep ripping Reddit threads and reading them aloud on TikTok and YouTube Shorts — Reddit would rather own that consumption pattern than hand it away for free. If you're mapping a customer journey involving Reddit-adjacent audiences, this is worth watching: it signals user behavior migrating toward lean-back, passive consumption even on a famously text-first platform.

Two creator economy stories worth pairing up. First, a talent agency founder is pushing back on the industry narrative that celebrity influence is dead — his argument is brands just keep misusing big names, conflating top-of-funnel awareness talent with bottom-of-funnel conversion talent, and paying for the wrong thing. His agency landed David Beckham, Alicia Keys and Stephen A. Smith for a campaign without paying talent fees, by structuring the deal around what those people actually wanted. If you're ever building an influencer strategy for a client, that funnel-position segmentation — awareness versus conversion — is the first question to ask before anyone talks budget.

Second, a smaller but tidier example: a marketer-turned-cooking-creator named Tyler Speegle runs his content brand like a growth function — A/B testing pacing and formats, and crucially, mining his own comments section for what his audience actually wants. That's how his seasoning product line got built, straight out of recurring audience questions. It's a nice reminder that comment sections are free, ongoing customer research, and that product development doesn't need a formal study when the demand signal is sitting right there in the replies.

Now, a genuinely juicy consumer strategy story. Dialogue AI ran a hundred-person study across five "better-for-you" drink brands — Liquid Death, OLIPOP, Health-Ade, GT's, and Ghia — and found a brutal retention problem: over a third of people who'd tried all five had dropped every single one. But here's the interesting part: retention had nothing to do with brand love or health claims. It came down to whether the drink actually replaced something specific. Swap out a named rival like Coke, and churn was 35 percent. Replace nothing — or just water — and churn shot to 73 percent. That's a fantastic lesson for any competitive mapping exercise: the real competitive set isn't "other drinks in this aisle," it's whatever specific habit you're actually displacing in someone's routine. Worth stealing that framework for any client trying to understand why trial isn't converting to loyalty.

And to close out the big one — Canva. Despite 265 million monthly users, nine straight years of profitability, and 25 percent revenue growth last quarter, Canva's valuation just got cut by up to 20 percent, dropping around 11 billion Australian dollars. Why? Not brand weakness — it's cost structure. Generative AI features turned out to be expensive to serve, and Canva's whole promise of "free, effortless design" only worked when marginal cost was basically zero. Now every AI interaction has a meter running, 800 million of them a month, and growth projections had to be slashed from 30 to 20 percent to rebuild the economics. The sharp distinction drawn here is between identity-based brand love, like Patagonia's, which survives a price hike, and relief-based brand love, like Canva's, which evaporates the moment a competitor removes friction faster. For any business case work you're doing where AI features are being added to a previously-free product, this is the cautionary tale: model out what happens to your value proposition and your unit economics the moment "free" starts costing you something per use.

It's basically a discipline for turning test results into things that can actually justify a business case, rather than another slide that says "statistically significant" and goes nowhere. And pairing nicely with that, there's a practical guide to auditing your LinkedIn conversion tracking — checking pixel firing, event parameters, timing issues — which matters more than it sounds, because if the tracking's broken, any business case built on that spend data is standing on sand.
