Morning, Niki. Here's your TLDR Marketing catch-up covering Friday the 31st of July through Monday the 3rd of August — a proper meaty one, so let's get into it.

Let's start on Reddit, because it's having a moment, good and bad. First, the good: Standing on Giants has laid out a proper playbook for brands doing Reddit community management properly — not just dropping in with a corporate account and hoping nobody notices. It starts with listening: mapping which subreddits your customers actually live in, what myths are circulating, what competitors are up to. Then comes relationship-building with moderators, disclosed brand accounts, training spokespeople not to sound like a press release. The bit that matters for you is the insight pipeline — routing what you learn on Reddit straight into product, support and marketing as genuine Voice of Customer research. If you're building a customer journey for a client, Reddit threads are basically unfiltered focus groups sitting there for free. Worth mapping which communities your client's customers frequent and what pain points keep resurfacing.

Now the less rosy Reddit news — the CEO, Steve Huffman, used the latest earnings report to take a swing at Google's AI Overviews, basically saying they're hurting content creators without delivering much value back. Reddit's stock dropped over twenty percent despite beating expectations, and there's talk they might walk away from their sixty-million-dollar licensing deal with Google. It's part of a bigger pattern — Pew Research found AI Overviews cut website referral traffic nearly in half. No direct action for you here, but it's a useful data point next time a client asks whether they should worry about AI eating their organic traffic. Short answer: yes, and Reddit's public spat with Google is exhibit A.

Speaking of AI search — there's a new geoSurge report that's genuinely useful ammunition. They found AI models search for brands they already remember from training data at over three times the rate of brands they don't. So if a brand's in the model's top ten memory, it gets surfaced in nearly fifty-six percent of relevant searches; if it's not remembered, that drops to about seventeen percent. And this holds across nine industries, most strongly in categories like automotive and finance where memory practically is the consideration set. The takeaway: this isn't something you fix with clever on-page SEO at query time. It's built through years of analyst coverage, partnerships, sustained category presence — the boring, slow stuff. If you're building a business case around AI visibility for a client, this is a good stat to reframe the conversation away from "let's optimise our content" toward "let's build durable category authority."

And staying in AI-and-shopping territory, there's a big stats roundup worth flagging because it's got real numbers behind a trend we've all been gesturing at. Forty-three percent of US online shoppers have used an AI assistant like ChatGPT or Gemini for product research in the last ninety days. A quarter of customers now treat AI as their primary source of product information, ahead of brand websites and reviews. Retail traffic from AI sources is up three hundred and fifty-seven percent year over year, and — this is the killer stat — AI-referred shoppers convert thirty-one percent better than other traffic. But here's the catch: eighty-six percent still verify the AI's recommendation somewhere else before buying, especially for anything over fifty dollars. So trust is conditional, not blind. For your work, this is a customer journey rewrite in progress — a meaningful chunk of purchase decisions are now forming before the customer ever lands on a brand or retailer site. Worth mapping, for any retail client, where in that AI-informed pre-decision phase you can actually show up and shape the choice, rather than just optimising the website they arrive at afterward.

Switching gears to something a bit more heartwarming — Churnet Valley Railway, a heritage steam railway in Staffordshire, just had its best year ever off the back of a website redesign. What I like about this one is the method, not just the outcome. User testing showed visitors ranked their site dead last against competitors — outdated, boring, confusing booking pages. Instead of a big-bang redesign, they tested page by page: homepage up five percent on conversions, a midweek lunch train page up forty-eight percent, and their Polar Express page got such a lift it sold out and they had to add extra carriages at Christmas. Each test fed learnings into the next. It's a clean, almost textbook example of using customer research to find the actual friction points — booking clarity, menu comprehension, availability information — and prioritising fixes by where the real pain is, rather than redesigning everything on a hunch. Good one to keep in your back pocket next time a client wants to skip research and jump straight to a redesign.

On the dating app front — Tinder is trying to get people off Tinder, sort of. They've launched Tinder Events globally after a strong pilot in LA, offering in-app sign-ups for things like cooking classes and mahjong nights, designed to get matches meeting in low-pressure group settings rather than one-on-one dates. The thinking, backed by actual anthropologists and psychologists, is that Gen Z is suffering what they're calling "social atrophy" from too much online interaction and wants easier, lower-stakes ways to meet people. It's rolling out to nine more cities now, twenty-six by September, with genuinely tailored local marketing — subway takeovers in New York, billboards in Kansas City. The strategic angle here is neat: Tinder's whole segmentation and marketing message is being built around a stated generational preference, not an assumed one. If you're ever building personas off "what we assume Gen Z wants" versus "what Gen Z has told us in research," this is a good real-world case for why the second one wins.

Now, a genuinely fascinating one for anyone thinking about go-to-market strategy — the story behind Cursor, the AI coding tool that just got acquired by SpaceX for sixty billion dollars. They did it with almost no traditional marketing. Their insight was that professional developers actively distrust marketing and only trust peer proof. So instead of building a marketing department, they built a five-minute "wow" product experience, shipped constantly and publicly, ran a bottom-up freemium model that let individual engineers at places like Shopify and OpenAI adopt it without procurement getting involved, and deployed three hundred community ambassadors running meetups and hackathons that doubled as tutorials and recruiting funnels. Competitors like Copilot couldn't copy it because they were relying on install-base advantage and static messaging, not community identity. This is a brilliant case study for skeptical B2B buyer segments generally — if you've got a client whose audience distrusts conventional selling, Cursor's approach is basically a template for building the business case around peer validation and frictionless product experience instead.

Quick detour into brand reputation — there's a nice long-read tracing the history of "subvertising," which is when activists hijack or spoof brand ads to expose hypocrisy — think tobacco billboard graffiti in the seventies right through to the group targeting Tesla and Meta today. A 2023 study confirmed it measurably damages brand perception and purchase intent. The pattern across fifty years is consistent: subvertising only really lands when it's exposing something true — a gap between what a brand says and what it does. Worth a competitor-watch line here: if you're assessing brand vulnerability for a client, it's less about whether activists might target them and more about whether there's a real values gap for activists to exploit in the first place.

On the people side — there's a piece arguing companies should be actively investing in employees' personal brands rather than fearing it as a flight risk, using Docebo's execs as the example. The strategic point that actually matters is the measurement angle: if you're advising a client on employee advocacy, don't just take it on faith that leadership visibility helps — it's worth pairing any personal-brand investment with actual research into how audiences perceive those leaders, and win-rate analysis to see whether it's actually correlating with deal velocity. Nice validation exercise for a business case.

Two quick ones on email. First, a Dubai agency making the case that open rate is a vanity metric and revenue is the only one that counts — they're segmenting subscribers into whales, potential whales, loyal customers, churned whales, and sending wildly different messaging and frequency to each, sometimes eight separate emails a day across segments for one client. It's a good reminder that lifetime-value segmentation, not blanket campaigns, is what actually moves revenue — a solid model to reference next time you're building an email or lifecycle strategy for a client.

Second email story is more of a warning than an insight. A Washington State court ruling means false urgency in email subject lines — "sale ends tonight" when it doesn't — is now proper legal exposure, with statutory damages of five hundred dollars per email, no cap. Costco just settled for fourteen million dollars. Nearly two hundred lawsuits are in motion against big retailers. The law's since been softened to a hundred dollars per email, but only going forward — everything before June stays under the higher bar. Practical takeaway for any retail or ecommerce client: audit whether subject line urgency claims actually reflect operational reality, because email creates a permanent, searchable paper trail in a way TV ads never did. That gap between promise and delivery isn't just a trust problem anymore, it's a legal one.

On the tech-and-money side — there's an interesting paradox in AI economics. Token prices have collapsed over ninety-five percent in three years, yet enterprise AI spending has more than doubled in six months, because cheaper tokens just mean everyone uses vastly more of them. The real margin problem is that AI inference is a variable cost that eats into margins in a way traditional software licensing never did — AI-native products are running fifty to sixty percent gross margins versus the eighty or ninety percent SaaS is used to. Companies are responding by shifting to usage-based pricing — Salesforce charging per action, Intercom charging ninety-nine cents per resolved query. If you're building a business case for an AI product client, this is the number to interrogate: don't assume seat-based pricing assumptions carry over, because the unit economics genuinely don't work the same way anymore.

And Google's quietly published its first proper guide for YouTube audio ads — short, cheap-to-produce spots for people listening to music or podcasts rather than watching video, capped at thirty seconds with minimal visual requirements. Worth a note for media planning conversations: it's a new, lower-cost surface, but it only works if you understand the listening context — background, half-attention — so any creative or targeting strategy needs research into how people actually use audio on YouTube, not just a video ad script read aloud.
