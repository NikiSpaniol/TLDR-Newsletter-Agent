Here's your download for Tuesday the fifteenth, Wednesday the sixteenth, and Thursday the seventeenth of September — three days of marketing news, and honestly, a decent chunk of it circles back to the same question: what happens when technology gets between a brand and an actual human being.

Let's start with the uncomfortable one. There's a growing pile of research on what short-form video is doing to our brains, and it's not flattering. A big 2026 meta-analysis of roughly seventy studies found short-form video use is linked to measurable declines in attention, impulse control, and memory — and the effect was actually stronger than the link to mental health problems, which is saying something. One researcher's own work found heavy scrollers had weaker episodic memory, which is basically your ability to remember your own life and build a coherent sense of self. Meta, for its part, just agreed to a seventeen billion dollar settlement that includes a two-hour daily cap and nighttime lockouts for young users — which is a quiet admission that yes, this is a problem, even while Zuckerberg publicly says the science isn't settled. For your world, the interesting angle isn't the doom, it's the gap it opens up: there's a real unmet customer need forming around control and intentional consumption. Any brand that can credibly position itself as the antidote to compulsive scrolling has a genuine differentiation story to tell.

Speaking of what people actually watch — YouTube just dropped a Culture and Trends Report that pushes back on the idea that mainstream culture is dead. Turns out it hasn't disappeared, it's just changed shape. Sixty-three percent of younger respondents still feel part of something bigger when they share interests online, and the report identifies three signals of a modern hit: it's everywhere, everyone gets it without explanation, and people actively participate rather than just watch. Creators and memes now rank almost as culturally significant as Hollywood celebrities — seventy-seven and seventy-eight percent awareness versus seventy-nine for A-listers. The poster child is "The Backrooms," a niche internet concept that snowballed into ninety million fan-made views and then an actual A24 movie that made a hundred and eighteen million dollars. If you're doing any journey mapping around content discovery or platform strategy, this is worth a proper read — it's a genuinely useful framework for how validation and participation now work.

Now, tying back to that human-versus-machine theme: there's a sharp little piece arguing that "proof of humanity" is the marketing opportunity of the moment. A Gartner survey found fifty-three percent of customers would consider switching providers if they found out AI was handling their service, and two-thirds of people under thirty react badly to AI in ads. Brands are leaning into this hard — Aerie's pledged never to use AI-generated people in its ads, Ramp put an actual human doing expense reports by hand in a Manhattan shop window and got eighty-five million views out of it. The practical playbook is simple: put real names and faces on your chat support, make an explicit anti-AI pledge if it's true, and create phone-free, human-first moments customers can point to. This is squarely a positioning question — if you're building competitive messaging for a client right now, "humanness" is a real axis to map competitors against, not just a nice-to-have.

Quick one while we're on trust-building: there's a webinar coming up with The Times' head of community, Rachel Duffy, on how to actually win on Reddit — not by broadcasting, but by building relationships with moderators and understanding subreddit culture from the inside. Worth a listen if you've got a client trying to break into community-first channels, because the lesson generalises well beyond Reddit: different platforms need genuinely different trust-building approaches, not one campaign copy-pasted everywhere.

Onto retail media, because this one's got a business-case trap baked into it. The market's projected to hit sixty-nine billion dollars in the US next year, up almost eighteen percent. But the article makes a sharp point: return on ad spend alone can lie to you. If a thousand dollars of retail media spend generates sales that would've happened anyway, that's not growth, that's just margin erosion dressed up as performance. The only way to know the difference is proper incrementality testing — randomised experiments, marketing mix models, or at minimum tracking new-customer sales against your organic baseline. If you've got a client pouring budget into Amazon or similar, this is exactly the assumption to stress-test in the business case before anyone celebrates the ROAS number.

Related theme, different flavour: a piece asking whether marketing teams have quietly turned into software teams without noticing. MIT research says ninety-five percent of enterprise AI projects fail, and internal builds fail more often than buying from vendors or bringing in experts. The fix is almost embarrassingly simple — spend thirty minutes sorting any AI initiative into buy, contract, or build before you touch it. Common problems, buy. Novel but one-off workflows, contract. Only build in-house for things your team deeply understands and will run repeatedly. This is a market-analysis exercise in disguise — figuring out what's genuinely differentiated versus commoditised is exactly the kind of thinking that should sit underneath any AI investment business case you're putting together.

Meanwhile, television's got its own attention problem. New Omdia research says three in four US viewers are on their phones while watching TV, and it's climbing fast even among older age groups — 55 to 64 year-olds went from forty-two to fifty-six percent dual-screening in just three years. The researcher behind it makes a good point: this isn't really a content problem, it's an attention allocation problem, and TV is now competing directly with vertical video on the same device it's sharing a room with. Anyone building a customer journey around video or TV advertising needs to draw that second screen into the picture, because pretending mobile and TV are separate environments just isn't true anymore.

On the B2B side, there's a nice trend piece on companies hiring senior editors to run thought leadership — and, crucially, measuring them on pipeline and win rates, not article counts. Workato's editorial lead reports into sales, not comms. The stat that stands out: seventy-one percent of "hidden buyers," the people who influence a deal without ever talking to sales, spend a big chunk of their week reading thought leadership. If you're mapping go-to-market for a B2B client, that's a direct nudge to make sure your journey accounts for buyers who never show up in a sales call at all.

On the personal branding front, there's a contrarian little piece worth thirty seconds: stop chasing virality, get known by the right hundred people. The idea is that three hundred senior people who instantly recognise your name beat a hundred thousand passive followers, because opportunities come from repeated visibility with decision-makers, not reach. It's a nice reminder that segmentation isn't just for customers — it applies to any influence strategy, including your own.

Now, a genuinely useful framework for anyone touching paid search. With Google's AI Overviews eating into clicks, one piece proposes sorting keywords into three buckets: Fight for high-intent commercial queries where owning the top slot is worth the premium, Influence for informational queries where the real goal is getting cited inside the AI answer, and Generate Demand for the zero-click queries where paid search can't do the job alone and you need to be building demand elsewhere, like YouTube. If you're advising a B2B or industrial client on budget allocation, this maps really cleanly onto different stages of their customer journey — it's worth stealing wholesale.

On the enterprise AI front, Salesforce and Nvidia just launched something called Koa — a reasoning model trained entirely on synthetic data rather than real customer information, specifically so nothing sensitive can ever leak. It's built to slot into Salesforce's Agentforce as an alternative to Claude or ChatGPT for things like customer service. The real story here is what it reveals about the market: enterprises have been nervous about handing sensitive data to the big AI labs, and Koa is a direct answer to that anxiety. If you're assessing AI vendors for a client, data sovereignty just became a real competitive axis, not just a compliance checkbox.

And Google's had a rough few days on the regulatory front. After a five hundred and thirty-four million euro EU fine, it's now surfacing competitor price-comparison sites ahead of its own tools for hotels and flights — which Google is dramatically calling the biggest quality reduction in its history, though it hasn't shown any data to back that up. Meanwhile forty-one of the top fifty US news sites lost traffic year over year, largely blamed on AI Overviews killing the need to click through. Worth flagging for any publisher or platform client — the economics of search traffic are shifting fast, and it's worth mapping how much of their funnel now depends on clicks that might simply stop coming.

A few quicker ones. Strava's sponsored research found sixty-four percent of marketers keep buying traditional ad formats out of pure habit, even though they know they underperform — and customers are getting sharper at tuning them out, with sixty-five percent more likely to engage with brands that get them physically active instead. If you're rethinking channel mix for a client, that's a decent data point for arguing engagement quality over raw reach.

Meta launched Meta One, a subscription bundle of creator and business tools across Instagram, Facebook, and WhatsApp, running from three dollars a month up to nearly five hundred for the top tier. Handy consolidation, but worth watching whether these paid features quietly become the new cost of visibility rather than a nice extra — that's a go-to-market cost question worth flagging early.

And if you ever need a benchmark for content pricing, there's a solid breakdown of what B2B writers actually charge in the UK — anywhere from thirty pence a word up to four thousand pounds for a technical white paper. The underlying point is a useful one for any buy-versus-build conversation: the price reflects research depth and subject expertise, and cheap or AI-generated content tends to cost more in the long run once you factor in editing and compliance risk in regulated sectors.

Last theme of the week: the creator economy's making peace with AI. Creators who once dreaded it are now using it to save ten-plus hours a week and boost income by twenty percent, though contracts are getting messy as brands try to extract creators' actual AI prompts and workflows. And Perplexity's taking a different tack entirely — hiring a creator marketing lead on a six-figure salary to run partnerships that look less like sponsorships and more like live product testing, using real creators to prove its tools work in actual workflows. If you're advising a product team on positioning in a crowded AI market, that's a nice case study in using creator partnerships as genuine user research rather than just reach-buying.

And that's your lot for this stretch — a week where the throughline was pretty clear: customers are getting more skeptical of anything that feels automated, and the brands winning attention are the ones proving there's a real person, and real judgement, behind the curtain. Catch you next time.
