Morning, Niki. Here's your marketing catch-up covering Friday the 4th and Monday the 7th of September — grab a coffee, this one's got range.

Let's start with the legal drama. A US federal judge has ruled that Google will not have to sell off its ad exchange, even though the same court found Google had illegally locked publishers into using it. The DOJ wanted a breakup; the judge said no. Ad tech watchers will keep an eye on the appeal, but for now, Google keeps its exchange intact. Filing that one under competitor-watch and moving on.

Speaking of platforms getting cleverer, let's talk Reddit — because there were two decent updates from them this week. First, they've launched fifteen-second Engaged Video Views, a new ad format that only bills advertisers when someone actually watches for fifteen seconds or finishes the video. Alpha testers saw completion rates jump seventy-one percent. The neat bit for us is the Split Testing feature — you can literally run the fifteen-second goal against their existing six-second one and see which timeframe your audience actually needs. If you've got a client debating whether their message lands in six seconds or needs a slow burn, that's your answer engine right there — a genuine input into creative strategy, not just a media buy.

And Reddit's Max campaigns — their automated targeting-and-budget tool — are now open to every advertiser, not just the beta group. Seventeen percent lower cost per acquisition, twenty-seven percent more conversions, and now API support plus a Smartly integration. Solid, unglamorous, worth knowing exists.

Now, a proper business-model story. There's a great "win report" out this week from a tutoring marketplace that lifted tutor sign-ups by fifteen percent — worth about twenty thousand extra applications a year — just by changing their recruitment landing page. Instead of a generic "register with us" form, they noticed most visitors were arriving straight from job-search sites. So they rewrote the page to look and feel like an actual job posting. Real student request, real job spec, benefits up front. That's it. The bigger lesson here is one worth stealing for any two-sided marketplace client: we spend so much energy optimising the demand side that the supply side — in this case, tutors — quietly becomes the real growth constraint. If you're ever building a business case for a marketplace client, that supply-side conversion funnel deserves its own line item, not an afterthought.

Sticking with the "who are we actually optimising for" theme — there's a meaty piece on how AI assistants decide what to cite. Turns out AI answers pull content that's, on average, twenty-six percent fresher than what ranks organically on Google, and half of all AI citations come from stuff updated in the last thirteen weeks. But — and this is the sting — that freshness advantage has a half-life of about four and a half weeks. So visibility isn't something you earn once, it's something you keep re-earning. Different platforms behave differently too: Perplexity and Grok reward freshness aggressively, ChatGPT wants genuinely new content, and Google's AI Overviews actually skew slightly older. The practical takeaway for us: before recommending a content refresh cadence to a client, map which AI platforms their actual customers are using. A freshness-first strategy makes sense if your buyers live in Perplexity; it's wasted effort if they're mostly hitting Google's Overviews.

On the subject of what to build versus what to license — there's a useful framework doing the rounds on build versus buy for marketing tools in what they're calling the "vibe code era," basically the age of AI-assisted development. The rule of thumb: build the tools that run on data you already own — attribution, dashboards, your own website content — and buy the ones requiring hard infrastructure, like email deliverability or compliance. If you're ever auditing a client's martech stack, that's a clean lens: which tools are genuine competitive advantage, and which are just commodity plumbing you're paying rent on.

Now, a genuinely important one for anyone thinking about customer journeys — a piece on how China is rethinking the entire path to purchase through creators. On platforms like Rednote, a shopper doesn't discover a product, then leave to buy it elsewhere — the whole thing happens in one place. Discovery, retargeting, livestream, purchase, all inside the app. China's livestream commerce market hit nine hundred billion dollars this year, with top hosts pulling in the equivalent of hundreds of millions in a single day. Europe's still fragmented — search here, retailer there, social somewhere else — but the direction of travel is obvious. The real strategic question for any client journey map right now isn't "which format should we use," it's "which platform actually owns the customer relationship end to end." That's a fundamentally different starting point for media planning.

Speaking of owning the relationship — Sticki Rolls, the collectible sticker brand, just proved something interesting about Gen Alpha marketing. They threw a pop-up with a nail salon in Soho, gave away sticker manicures, and picked up twenty thousand new YouTube subscribers and a fifty percent spike in search interest in a week. The bit worth noting for journey mapping: these purchases are rarely solo decisions — parents are in the room, often paying, sometimes just as delighted by the stickers as the kids. Any positioning work for youth brands needs to account for that dual audience, not just the target demo on paper.

And while we're talking about brands that found a gap nobody else was serving — SKIMS. Everyone assumes it's a Kardashian halo-effect story, but the more interesting read is the market gap she spotted: shapewear had been stuck in shame-based, beige, limited-size territory for decades. SKIMS repositioned it as visible lifestyle wear, nine nude shades, sizes XXS to 5X, and built distribution on Kim's own following rather than paid media. If you're ever building a case for entering a "boring," incumbent-heavy category, this is the template — the win wasn't celebrity, it was spotting an unmet need hiding in plain sight and being the first to actually design for it.

Quick one on TikTok — they're adding voice comments, polls, and photo carousels to comment sections, trying to turn them into hangout spaces rather than feedback boxes. Nothing strategic to chew on there, just useful to know your clients' comment sections are about to get noisier.

But TikTok's bigger move this week is on the ads side — they've launched something called Ads MCP and an Agentic Hub, which basically lets AI agents plug straight into TikTok Ads and run campaigns, analysis, and creative workflows without a human toggling between tools. The interesting shift here isn't the automation itself — reporting and anomaly detection were always going to get automated. It's that competitive advantage is moving from "who has platform access" to "whose proprietary playbook is encoded into the AI." For any client with a strong-performing paid social team, this is the moment to actually document their best decision-making frameworks — because that institutional know-how is about to become the differentiator, not the media buy itself.

If you want a shortcut for that kind of competitive homework, there's a platform called Dusted worth bookmarking — it's a library of real marketer-submitted case studies, organised by discipline and by outcome, so you can see exactly how peer companies did customer research, built GTM strategy, or launched products. Genuinely useful for competitive benchmarking without reinventing the wheel.

Which ties nicely into a spicier piece: an argument that SEO agencies, as we know them, are basically obsolete. The reasoning is that their core product — churning out content — can now be done by AI at a fraction of the cost, and most agencies have just quietly rebranded their writers as "AEO specialists" without changing anything underneath. The author's bet on what survives: agencies that do genuine jobs-to-be-done customer research and competitive positioning work, and agencies that do real PR — because AI models are now citing sources based on third-party credibility, which makes earned media newly valuable. Worth flagging to any client currently paying an agency purely for content volume — ask what strategic thinking they're actually getting for that fee.

On the customer research front, there's a sharp new study on Gen Z loyalty that's worth remembering next time someone claims young consumers have zero brand loyalty. Sixty-four percent say they're loyal most or all of the time — but only eleven percent feel a brand truly understands them, and that small group is nine times more likely to stay loyal than the rest. Price and quality still dominate over values-alignment by a wide margin. And there's real segmentation hiding in the data: American Gen Z feel far more understood by brands than Canadian Gen Z, and customer service matters a lot more to older Gen Z than younger. If you're building a Gen Z strategy for a client, this is basically a warning against treating "Gen Z" as one homogenous market — geography and age cohort both change the loyalty equation.

Two related pieces worth pairing together — both about reputation showing up where you least control it. First, negative Reddit threads are increasingly what shows up in Google results and AI answers, and the advice is to stop trying to suppress them and instead show up as a real human, selectively, helpfully, and consistently — treating Reddit as a genuine customer touchpoint rather than a PR liability. Second, there's a nice tactical piece on public booking forms getting flooded by bots within weeks of launch, because AI website builders generate slick forms with zero spam protection baked in. Both stories land on the same point: map every place a prospective customer actually encounters your brand — including the unglamorous ones like a contact form or a Reddit thread — because that's where trust is quietly won or lost.

A lighter one worth your time on a train journey: Glossier and Dishoom's Roya Shariat talking about her career. She landed her first Glossier job by literally pitching Emily Weiss on the street with a five-slide deck showing that consumers were shifting toward value-led purchasing. It's a nice reminder that a sharp piece of customer research, packaged simply, can build a business case for an entire new role or initiative — not just a new product.

Two more before we wrap. Google Research published a study on why large language models forget things — turns out they store the vast majority of facts they're trained on but fail to recall a third of them, mostly because of what's called the "reversal curse," where a model struggles if you ask a fact backwards from how it learned it. For anyone doing AI visibility work, the fix is to state your key facts in multiple directions and multiple places, so they become "popular" enough for the model to reliably retrieve — and the same logic applies to auditing competitors, since a visibility gap might be missing content, or it might just be a retrieval problem.

And finally, a compliance note worth flagging to any client leaning hard into influencer marketing: disclosure rules remain a mess on both sides of the Atlantic. A recent EU sweep found ninety-seven percent of influencers posting commercial content, but only twenty percent disclosing it properly. Penalties range from FTC fines to four percent of turnover under EU rules, and new class actions are naming brands as co-defendants alongside the influencers themselves. If a client's acquisition strategy leans on influencer partnerships, this is worth a compliance check before it's a headline.
