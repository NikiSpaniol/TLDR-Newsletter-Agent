Hey Niki, here's your TLDR Marketing catch-up covering Friday the 28th and Monday the 31st of August. Grab a coffee, this one's got some good stuff.

Let's start with packaging, because there's a genuine generational rebellion happening on shelves right now. Millennial brands spent a decade perfecting that clean, minimalist, Instagram-friendly look — think Glossier, think Warby Parker, muted colours and lots of white space. Gen Z grew up drowning in that aesthetic, and now they're actively rejecting it for stuff that's louder, weirder, and more illustrative. Brands like OLIPOP, Fishwife, and Graza are winning by making "healthy" feel desirable instead of austere, embracing maximalist detail, and even leaning into intentionally imperfect, handmade looks. The takeaway isn't "use bright colours" — it's that authenticity now reads as personality, not restraint. If you're building segmentation for a Gen Z-facing client, this is a real emotional driver worth testing in customer research: does your brand feel like it's performing minimalism, or does it actually have a point of view?

Quick one on the state of the internet: Pew Research dug into how much web content is now AI-written. As of this January, about one in ten dot-com pages show signs of AI authorship, up dramatically since ChatGPT launched. Fun bonus fact — em dashes are up, Oxford commas are up 63%, and everyone's suddenly obsessed with the word "delve." Mostly a curiosity, but a handy gut-check next time a piece of content feels a little too smooth.

Now, something genuinely useful for anyone building media plans: TextNow ran a 26-week experiment to figure out when YouTube awareness campaigns actually start driving lower-funnel results. The headline finding — 87% of the measured lift showed up after the first two months, with early readings basically flat. If they'd judged the campaign on a four-week read, they'd have called it a failure. For any customer journey work you're doing, this is a great real-world data point: upper-funnel channels often have long payback horizons, and if a business case assumes fast attribution, that assumption needs stress-testing against the channel's actual lag time, not the reporting cadence someone's used to.

Sticking with frameworks — there's a new one floating around called the "inevitable brand," from branding strategist Zlatina Petrova. The interesting bit for us is her three-level model: brands compete on functional, experiential, or transformational grounds. It's a clean way to map where a client's actual battleground is. Are competitors fighting over features, over how the experience feels, or over what owning the product says about the customer? Worth stealing next time you're mapping competitive positioning — it forces a client to pick a lane instead of vaguely trying to win on all three.

Speaking of picking lanes under pressure — AirOps surveyed over 300 marketing leaders and found a pretty brutal resource squeeze. Seventy-five percent of teams have higher targets this year, but only 43% got any budget increase, and fewer than a quarter got their full ask approved. Teams are cutting agency spend, freezing backfill hires, and going smaller and more senior. And here's the kicker: teams that did get fully funded were 2.3 times more likely to grow. If you're building a business case for headcount or tooling, that stat is basically free ammunition — full funding correlates directly with growth, not just comfort. Also worth flagging: 90% of leaders are chasing AI, but only 7% call their AI maturity advanced. Lots of clients are investing ahead of their actual capability, which is exactly the kind of gap competitor analysis should be probing.

Two Reddit stories worth pairing up. First, Reddit has quietly become an SEO powerhouse — it ranks for something like 166 million keywords, over 10 million of them in the number one spot, thanks to those licensing deals with Google and OpenAI. The practical play here: use Semrush's Keyword Gap tool to see where Reddit outranks you in your category. It surfaces three things — pages you could optimise to beat Reddit, content Reddit covers that you don't, and FAQ-style content customers are currently only finding on Reddit threads. That's a genuinely actionable content gap analysis you could run for a client this week.

But — and this is the twist — Reddit's visibility inside ChatGPT just fell off a cliff. Citations dropped from 3.8% to 0.5% in a couple of weeks, an 86% collapse, with OpenAI shrugging and saying they don't fix visibility levels. This happened once before, a year ago, and it was traced back to a Google parameter change rather than any grand strategy. The lesson for anyone who built an AI-visibility strategy around "just get cited on Reddit" — that channel is a lot less stable than it looks. If a client's counting on it, it's worth tracking whether that lost visibility is landing on a competitor's domain instead, because that's a live competitive-shift worth watching.

On the AI-and-trust front, Ahrefs published a nice piece on how they use AI for every article without it turning into slop. Their definition of slop isn't "AI was involved" — it's "nobody applied judgement." They build in human checkpoints before drafting, at the outline stage, and before publishing, and every article has a named owner who has to stand behind it. It's a useful mental model if you're mapping a content journey for a client adopting AI: the question isn't where to add automation, it's where human judgement is non-negotiable, and locking that in explicitly rather than letting it erode.

On the growth side, there's a fun founder story from Ali Abouelatta, who grew a product called Lazyweb to over 50,000 signups with zero paid marketing. The tactics that worked were oddly specific — removing the email requirement for guest accounts lifted activation by half, and a free MCP server, essentially a plug so AI agents could access the platform faster, doubled daily active users. His broader insight is that if your real customer is increasingly an AI agent acting on someone's behalf, you should be optimising for what agents actually want — quality, speed, cost — not classic marketing levers. Worth filing away for any client whose product might end up being used by an agent rather than a human.

Also useful for content teams: someone built a "Helpful Content Grader," a GPT tool that scores web pages against Google's own 32-question helpful-content checklist and spits out a letter grade plus a prioritised fix list. If you're validating whether a client's content actually meets the search intent you identified in research, rather than just guessing, this is a nice concrete audit layer to bolt onto that work.

And for anyone stitching together AI agents across sales, product, and marketing teams, there's a framework doing the rounds called the "Shared Agent Brief." The core problem it solves is boring but real — different people give the AI conflicting instructions, and nobody's agreed on who actually has authority over which decision. The framework maps five things: shared objective, who inputs what, who decides, where AI must stop, and how decisions get recorded. If you're ever designing a GTM workflow involving AI for a client, that decision-rights map is the bit people skip and then regret.

Quickly — Sony Music and Warner have now sued Anthropic, alleging it illegally torrented and scraped copyrighted lyrics and sheet music to train Claude. This follows the $1.5 billion Bartz settlement earlier this year. Just another log on the AI-copyright fire — worth knowing, nothing to action yet.

And a nice culture note: creative strategist Laura Cameron has coined "Women in SPAM" — Social Media, PR, Advertising and Marketing — as a rallying identity for women tired of having serious marketing careers reduced to "posting on Instagram." No one calls finance people "finance boys," as she points out. Fair.

Google also dropped some creative guidance for YouTube advertisers worth a glance if you touch video briefs: creative quality drives 49% of campaign ROI, human voice in ads lifts conversions 12%, and showing your brand in the first five seconds adds another 4%. Competitor-watch territory if any client's YouTube creative is still leading with a logo bar and no human face.

On the design-agency side, Unfold shared what it's actually like working with giants like Facebook, Merck, and Ripple — the design work itself doesn't change, but everything around it does: procurement, NDAs, months-long approval chains, and stakeholders who all want something slightly different. Once you clear the vendor-approval hurdle though, it apparently unlocks years of work. More of a "brace yourself" than a strategy note, but relevant if you're ever scoping enterprise engagements.

Now, one I'd actually sit with for a while: a sharp piece arguing that retail media is being mislabeled as brand building. Walmart's ad business grew 43% last quarter to $6.4 billion, and it runs at roughly 70% margin versus about 4% for their core retail business — so of course everyone's piling in. But the argument is that retail media only reaches people already standing in the aisle ready to buy. It's bottom-funnel, defensive spend dressed up in brand-building language. And an ANA survey found 90% of that money is coming out of other budgets, often brand budgets, not incremental spend. If a client's shifting money into retail media and calling it brand investment, that's exactly the kind of channel-attribution assumption worth pressure-testing in a business case before it quietly hollows out their long-term growth spend.

Last one — Validity surveyed 500 marketers and found a slightly alarming stat: 91% know clean data matters for AI, but only 21% think their CRM data is actually ready for it. Sixty-two percent say bad data has probably already cost them revenue, and that number jumps to 92% among VPs when you ask if they've acted on an AI recommendation they later suspected was wrong. As more clients hand decisions to agentic AI, this is worth keeping in your back pocket — it's the kind of unglamorous data-hygiene issue that quietly wrecks otherwise good strategy.
