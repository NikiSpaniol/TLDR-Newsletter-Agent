Now, one from the archives that's a genuinely great strategy story. Back in 2013, DJI launched its first drone using a GoPro mount, riding GoPro's brand to legitimize the whole category without doing any camera R&D themselves. When GoPro's founder came back in 2014 offering a formal partnership — two-thirds of the profits, no less — DJI's CEO turned it down flat. His logic: the moat wasn't the camera, it was the flight technology. DJI shipped four drone generations in the next thirty months. GoPro's own drone, the Karma, got recalled sixteen days after launch and was dead by 2018. There are two things worth pulling from this for competitive analysis: first, always sanity-check whether a partnership offer is undervaluing what you actually think your core advantage is — DJI clearly decided GoPro didn't understand its own value. And second, iterative speed during a rival's stumble can build a lead that's basically impossible to claw back.

Polaroid's having a moment too, and it's a lovely positioning case study. A quick, very concrete one on conversion design. An editing services company removed their prominent "Get Quote" button from the homepage header and conversions went up 39%. Turns out the button was rushing people into a detailed quote form before they'd built any trust or understanding — so stripping it out forced them through service descriptions and credentials first, and quote requests further down the page actually converted better. It's a clean little proof point for customer-journey thinking: sometimes the aggressive early call-to-action is the thing killing your funnel, not saving it. Good one to keep in your back pocket when a client insists on more CTAs, not fewer.

Related note, different angle: there's a nice piece called "stop marketing to strangers," which argues most brands over-invest in acquisition and under-invest in the people who already know them — no clear next step for returning visitors, no advanced content for long-term followers, broken promises like outdated hours or dead links. On the research side — there's a nice, tightly scoped study on AI chatbots and sales. Across four experiments with over 1,300 people, chatbots boosted purchase intent by nearly 45% for products that are genuinely hard to evaluate, like clothing with lots of fit variables. But for simple products with a small range of options — think a basic vitamin line — chatbots actually hurt conversion. Give them a large assortment of easy-to-evaluate products, though, and chatbots help again, by managing choice overload. The business case implication is straightforward: before anyone recommends a client invest in chatbot tech, you'd want to map their catalog against evaluation complexity and assortment size first — that's genuinely the variable that determines ROI here, not the tech itself.
