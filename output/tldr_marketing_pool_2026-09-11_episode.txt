Morning, Niki. Here's your TLDR Marketing catch-up, covering Tuesday the 8th, Wednesday the 9th, and Thursday the 10th of September. Grab a coffee, it's a meaty one.

Let's start with the traffic story, because it's got a genuinely useful nugget buried in it. Semrush pulled U.S. web traffic data comparing July last year to July this year, and the headline is that Google and YouTube still look untouchable, up double digits each. But the real mover is ChatGPT, which cracked the top ten with over a billion monthly visits and 48% year-on-year growth, waltzing straight past Bing and DuckDuckGo, both of which are bleeding traffic for very different reasons. Here's the bit that should worry anyone doing attribution work, though: as more of that discovery happens inside AI chat interfaces, referrer data just disappears. One publisher in the piece saw direct traffic jump from 62% to 78% of sessions, and most of that unexplained bump is almost certainly ChatGPT users arriving with no trail behind them. So if you're building a customer journey map and leaning hard on GA4 to tell you where people first heard of a brand, that map is getting blurrier by the month. Worth pairing it with something like SparkToro or GWI, or honestly just asking customers directly, because the platform data alone is going dark.

Speaking of measurement, there's a lovely piece on how Netflix actually proves its billboards and TV sponsorships work, since you obviously can't A/B test a billboard on an individual. They use geo experiments — randomising by city or region rather than by person — through an internal tool called Quasimodo, and they treat every campaign pause and relaunch as another data point rather than a one-off test. The interesting takeaway isn't the statistics, it's the organisational discipline: someone senior has to commit to a holdout region, protect that test window from being trampled by a national campaign, and actually repeat the test on a schedule instead of running it once and calling it settled. If you're ever building a business case that leans on "this channel drives incremental sales," this is the standard to hold it to — a market-level causal read that's less than two years old, not a story someone told a CFO once at a budget meeting.

Big platform news: Nvidia is buying Hugging Face, the open-source hub that hosts three million AI models and eighteen million developers, for nearly thirteen billion dollars. It's not really about revenue — Hugging Face only pulls in about $150 million a year. It's about ecosystem gravity. Nvidia wants every developer building on open models to be reaching for its chips by default, which quietly expands its addressable market way past the handful of enterprise giants and out into thousands of smaller shops experimenting with AI. If you're mapping the competitive landscape for anything chip- or infrastructure-adjacent, this is a axis worth tracking: who owns the developer relationship, not just who owns the silicon.

Now, a business-case story that's a bit of a gut punch. Someone dissected an ecommerce account boasting an 11x return on ad spend, and it turns out every single order was losing money. A £100 order nets down to £60 after VAT, then loses another £28 to returns, then gets eaten alive by cost of goods, fulfilment, shipping and payment fees — leaving a negative 39 pence per order. The 11x figure was also blending brand campaigns, which would've converted anyway, with much weaker non-brand spend, which flattered the average. If you're building or reviewing a business case for paid media, this is your reminder to rebuild conversion values as actual contribution margin, split brand from non-brand, and never trust a blended ROAS to tell you the truth about profitability.

And while we're on measurement myths, there's a sharp little piece arguing that plenty of companies buy expensive custom attribution models before they're remotely ready for one. The author's rule of thumb: if the analysis isn't going to change a real budget decision, don't build it. You want at least one channel that's already proven to work, a genuinely complex multi-channel mix, and clear allocation decisions sitting there waiting to be made — otherwise you're just paying a lot of money to confirm what you already suspected.

Onto the AI-answers economy, because this one's moving fast. OpenAI's ChatGPT Ads have hit a billion dollars in annualised revenue after roughly two hundred days, now live in over forty countries. The structural shift is that paid results now sit inside the actual conversational answer, right next to organic citations, in one single frame — and only the paid slot is guaranteed. Google's doing the same trick inside AI Overviews and AI Mode. That quietly undercuts the whole premise of answer-engine optimisation, the idea that earning a citation is durable, unbought value — because a CFO can now directly compare a guaranteed paid placement against an organic mention that might vanish next week. Worth running that comparison yourselves for any client leaning heavily on AEO: model the ROI of paid adjacency against the volatility of earned citations, because right now only one of those is something you can actually buy with confidence.

A quick one from the world of platform strategy: the "aftermarket premium." Think AMG, Singer restomods, Red Hat, Cursor — businesses built entirely on modifying someone else's platform. Owners of the underlying platform have four options: coexist, fight, license, or acquire, and the article's watching two new categories forming right now, espresso machines and robotics. If you're ever assessing a platform play for a client, this is a genuinely useful lens — ask whether the preconditions exist for a profitable modder ecosystem to spring up around them, and whether they should be encouraging that or shutting it down.

There's also a really good essay called "Seeing Like a State," using eighteenth-century Prussian forestry as a warning about over-optimising for what's easy to measure. Prussia turned messy old-growth forest into neat, measurable rows of trees, yields soared, and then collapsed twenty to thirty percent once the invisible soil ecosystem gave out. The author draws a straight line to corporate dashboards and stack-ranking systems that measure performance beautifully while quietly killing the informal trust and knowledge that made the place function in the first place. For anyone building customer journey maps or research programmes, it's a good gut check: ask what your measurement system simply can't see, because that blind spot might be exactly what's driving retention.

A few quicker hits now. Cursor ran a cheeky little campaign where individual employees personally offered free access to anyone who cancelled a rival service and sent a screenshot. It worked brilliantly because it felt like a personal favour rather than a company promo, and it quietly handed Cursor a stream of competitive intelligence — exactly why people left, and what they'd been paying. If you ever need a low-cost way to gather real competitor pricing and churn reasons, that's a tactic worth stealing.

There's also a nice piece revisiting Drew Eric Whitman's "Life-Force 8" framework — the idea that great ads don't invent desire, they borrow it, tapping into one of eight deep human wants like survival, status or fear reduction. Old Listerine and Charles Atlas ads are the classic examples. Useful gut-check for any positioning work: figure out which of those eight desires your product is actually serving before you write another feature-led headline.

Pinterest published a "wellbeing" report positioning itself as the anti-doomscroll platform for teens — more searching and saving, less broadcasting, comments off by default for younger users. If you're doing journey mapping in a youth-facing category, it's a decent data point on how design choices shape genuine engagement versus just time-on-platform.

On trials: new data from Superwall across ten thousand-plus apps shows seven-day free trials beat three-day trials by about ten percent overall — though the gap is much bigger on Android than iOS, and reverses entirely for iOS users in North America. If a client's optimising trial length, don't assume one answer travels across markets.

And engagement metrics are shifting fast — Instagram likes on carousels are down 73% year on year, Reels down 81%, because algorithms now care about watch time and completion, not hearts and comments. If you're reporting social performance up the chain, it might be time to swap the vanity metrics for watch-through data before someone asks why the likes fell off a cliff.

Two quick industry notes to close out the AI side of things. It was a genuinely chaotic week for model releases — OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Meta all shipped new versions within days of each other, and enterprise teams are increasingly exhausted just trying to keep up, a phenomenon people are now calling "model fatigue." And streaming prices, for what it's worth, have been rising three times faster than inflation since 2022 — Apple TV has tripled in price, Disney+ is up 172%, and yes, Gen Z really is rotating subscriptions on and off to dodge the bill.
