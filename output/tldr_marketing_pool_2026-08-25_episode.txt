Hey Niki, here's your TLDR Marketing catch-up for Friday the 21st and Monday the 24th of August — a bit of a bumper edition, so let's get straight into it.

First up, a genuinely interesting power shift between Reddit and OpenAI. Remember how Reddit became the poster child for selling its user content to AI companies? Well, that relationship just got a lot cooler. OpenAI quietly switched how ChatGPT pulls in web results, moving to something called a "site operator" search, which grabs content from specific websites rather than crawling the open web. The upshot: Reddit citations in ChatGPT answers have crashed from about 4.5% of responses down to roughly 0.5%. Reddit's response is telling — it's now converting user posts and comments into AI-generated videos and podcasts on its own platform, basically trying to become its own AI content engine rather than someone else's training data. No direct action for you here, just a good one to file away: platforms that got rich licensing data to AI companies are realising that relationship can be switched off overnight.

Now, one that's much more up your street. There's a great piece on how Gen Alpha — so, kids roughly seven to fourteen — are reshaping back-to-school spending. The headline number: these kids now directly influence 48% of parental spending, and during back-to-school season that number climbs to nearly 60%, ahead of school supply lists and even sales promotions. The average family's laying out $922 for that initial back-to-school shop, which then snowballs into about $7,000 of school-related spending across the year. And it's not passive influence either — a quarter of under-14s are making independent online purchases without even owning a credit card, and over half of second and third graders say social media is steering what they buy. The strategic point here is worth sitting with: this isn't "market to the parent, kid tags along" anymore. You've effectively got two customers inside one household — the parent controlling the wallet and the kid controlling the preference — moving through different channels, with the parent still shopping in-store but the kid living on social and marketplaces. If you're mapping a customer journey for a retail or family-brand client, this is the moment to stop treating the household as a single persona and start treating it as its own mini ecosystem with a blast radius — because the purchase decision is being negotiated between two people who don't shop the same way at all.

Sticking with the customer-journey theme, there's a nice practical framework doing the rounds on personalization — specifically, how you tell when it's crossed from helpful into creepy. The suggestion is three quick tests before you activate any personalized experience: would the customer reasonably expect you to be using this data here, does it actually make the experience better, and is the value you're creating worth the sensitivity of what you're using to create it. Score each one high, medium, or low, and you've got a simple decision matrix instead of just switching on everything the martech stack technically allows. This maps really cleanly onto journey work — next time you're building out touchpoints for a client, it's worth explicitly asking at each stage: what does the customer actually want us to know about them right now, versus what do we know that just makes them feel watched.

Here's one that should make you pause before signing off on any promotional mechanic. New research out of the Journal of Marketing Research has found that conditional promotions — the "spend X to get Y off" or "sign up for two years to unlock this discount" type deals — can actually shrink total sales rather than grow them. The mechanism's quite elegant, actually: a big precondition, like a long contract, filters out a lot of customers, while a big discount simultaneously makes the ones who are still eligible less willing to just pay full price anyway. So you lose people at both ends. The advice is to keep the precondition light, target people who'll clear it easily, and be selective about who you even show the offer to, so you're not disappointing shoppers who were never going to qualify. If you're ever building a business case around a promotional campaign, this is a genuinely useful sense-check — model the precondition cost and the discount size together, not separately, because it's the combination that kills conversion, not either one alone.

Now a really nice real-world case study — the Washington Post has had to completely rethink how it acquires paying subscribers, because AI search is quietly strangling its organic traffic. Pew Research found AI Overviews and tools like ChatGPT have cut click-through rates from search by nearly 47%. Instead of doubling down on traditional discount-led ads, the Post's paid team started boosting organic, journalist-led video as paid content on Meta — essentially putting money behind stories that were already resonating rather than manufacturing conversion-optimized ads. Over six months, that approach delivered four times the click-through rate of their old direct-response campaigns. The read here: audiences, including younger ones who supposedly have no attention span, respond to content that feels native and human, not engineered. For anyone doing acquisition strategy work, this is a strong signal that as organic discovery breaks down, the winning move isn't more aggressive paid messaging — it's finding your best organic storytelling and putting spend behind that instead.

A few quick ones now. Meta's put out a report making the case for women's sports as the next big brand opportunity — it's a three billion dollar industry growing four and a half times faster than men's sports, with huge engagement numbers on Instagram and around WNBA and women's soccer content. Their pitch is that sponsorship territory here is still up for grabs, unlike men's sports where the good spots are all locked in. Worth knowing as a competitor-watch item if any clients are weighing sponsorship budgets.

And Similarweb has launched a tool that tracks ads showing up inside ChatGPT and Google's AI Mode — apparently over a quarter of ChatGPT responses and nearly 30% of eligible Google AI Mode queries already carry a sponsored placement. Useful bit of competitive intelligence plumbing if you ever need to check what competitors are doing in that space, but nothing to action today.

Also on the radar: New York has officially overtaken the San Francisco Bay Area as the biggest tech talent market for the first time in thirteen years of CBRE tracking this, thanks to AI hiring in finance and a wave of AI startups, while the Bay Area shrank on the back of layoffs at Meta, Block and Amazon. Bay Area still wins on overall quality when you factor in salaries and graduates, but the talent map is shifting.

And a fun cultural one — young people are ditching apps and doomscrolling for actual in-person meetups: chess clubs, birding groups, silent reading nights, mahjong. Trivia nights are up over 400% year on year. It's basically a loneliness-driven swing back to analog socialising. No client action needed, but a good one to keep in your back pocket if you're ever thinking about experiential or community marketing trends.

Right, back to the meaty stuff — creator marketing had two really solid pieces this edition. First, CreatorIQ surveyed over five thousand creators and found that brand suitability is now the number one thing brands look for in a partnership, while follower count has fallen to dead last, eighth out of eight. And yet — brands still pay based on follower count. That mismatch is creating real tension: over half of creators with 500,000-plus followers say they feel caught between what their audience wants and what the brand is paying them to say. If you're building a creator framework for a client, the takeaway is to formalize audience-and-values alignment as the primary screening criterion, and treat follower count as a pricing input, not a selection input — because right now a lot of brands are quietly doing the opposite and diluting authenticity in the process.

Second, there's a great piece on actually measuring what review content and creator partnerships are worth. The core idea is distinguishing genuine new customer acquisition from what the author calls "parasitic" affiliate commissions — paying a creator for a sale that customer was already going to make anyway. A lot of brands are effectively paying three times for the same relationship through PR, affiliate fees, and amplification, and mistaking the resulting noise for organic proof it's working. The fix is incrementality analysis — would this person have bought anyway — layered onto journey analysis of where in the funnel that creator actually influenced the decision. If you're building a business case for a creator program, this is exactly the kind of rigor worth insisting on, otherwise you risk baking margin leakage straight into the model.

On the platform side, YouTube is reportedly throwing millions at top creators to stay exclusive, and quietly punishing anyone who takes a Netflix deal by pulling marketing support and event access. Netflix, meanwhile, is aggressively poaching creator talent to build its own slate. It's the streaming subscription-fatigue problem all over again, just with creators instead of shows — a good one to watch if any client's content strategy depends on a specific creator staying put on a specific platform.

Now, a couple of brand-strategy pieces that are genuinely worth your time. There's a sharp piece called "Everyone Knows You, Do They Care," about a technique where you map a target audience's social follows to see which brands and communities actually cluster together in people's attention. One large, well-known client brand ran this exercise and simply didn't show up on the map at all — high awareness, but nobody cared enough to follow it. The reframe is smart: rather than treating that as a failure, the strategist argues it's a blank canvas, because the brand isn't tied down to any cultural corner and can choose one deliberately, rather than ending up what they call "culturally homeless" by trying to appeal to everyone at once. That's a genuinely useful diagnostic for competitive positioning work — next time you're mapping a client against competitors, it's worth checking not just who they're aware of, but who they actually care enough about to associate with, because those can be very different maps.

And there's a lovely interview with Dennis Wang, who built lululemon's accessories division from nothing to over a billion dollars. His whole philosophy boils down to a framework he calls C.P.R. — understand the Customer, engineer the Product around their specific friction, generate Relevancy in the experience. The Everywhere Belt Bag is his go-to example: it worked because it solved a very real, very unglamorous problem — needing your hands free with just your essentials — a need that exploded during the pandemic. His advice for any new category launch is blunt: absolute clarity on the customer persona has to come before a single strategy or execution decision, because everything else downstream gets dictated by it. That's about as clean a validation as you'll get for leading business cases with customer definition first, rather than backing into it after the product's already built.

One more brand one, smaller but tidy — a Brooklyn design studio built the entire brand system for a new gyoza and cocktail bar, Lucky Parlor, around a strategy first, logo second approach. They landed on "friendly but premium, playful but elevated," then built a set of repeatable visual hooks — a gyoza mark, custom type, illustrations — that had to work even with the logo removed entirely. The test-your-brand-without-the-logo idea is a genuinely handy diagnostic to steal for any positioning workshop: if the client's brand can't survive without its logo doing all the work, the positioning probably isn't distinctive enough yet.

And finally, a fun bit of trivia to close on — researchers at the University of Vienna reckon they've found one of the earliest examples of branding, in Bronze Age pottery from Saudi Arabia, about 3,200 years old. Consistent colours, consistent shapes, centralized production, deliberately exported to be recognizable across communities — basically all the ingredients of a modern brand, just in clay instead of packaging. Nice reminder that everything you're doing with positioning and recognition today is, genuinely, a very old game.
