Let's start with the thing you'll actually want to open a tab for: Ahrefs just launched something called Brand Radar. It tracks how often a brand gets mentioned or cited inside AI-generated answers — so ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity, Copilot, Google's AI Overviews, the lot. You can instantly benchmark any brand against nearly half a billion pre-collected prompts, or set up custom prompts to track the exact questions your buyers are asking. Why this matters for you: it's basically a new lens for customer research. Instead of guessing how people search, you can now see the actual questions they're putting to AI before they ever hit a website — and spot the gap between how customers are asking and how your client's brand is showing up, or not showing up, in the answer. That's a genuinely useful input for a customer journey map, and worth testing on a live account before a competitor beats you to the insight.

Related thread: there's new research on YouTube influencer sponsorships and AI citations. When a sponsored YouTube video gets cited inside an AI answer, the sponsor's brand name shows up in that answer about 40% of the time, versus 32% when it isn't cited — so getting cited is genuinely worth something. The catch is the effect only really fires on Google's AI Mode, AI Overviews, Perplexity and Gemini — ChatGPT and Copilot barely touch YouTube at all — and the boost fades fast, dropping over 40% within a month. Also, it only works if the sponsorship is written into the video description, not just said out loud. So if you're building a business case for influencer spend, this is a new assumption to test: is the sponsorship language actually in the description box? That's now a deal-term worth negotiating, not an afterthought.

And it's not just influencer content — brands are starting to advertise directly to AI agents. Time magazine has teamed up with an ad-tech platform called Mobian to serve specially formatted, fact-rich pages to a list of about seventy AI bots and agents, essentially feeding them brand-verified information before they ever crawl the real webpage. Wayfair's doing something similar by enriching product data for something called the Universal Commerce Protocol, Google's new standard for making products understandable to agents. The logic is simple: agents are scraping once and reusing that memory for future recommendations, so if you don't feed them the right facts, they'll either get it wrong or get it from someone else. Worth mapping into any agent-era customer journey work — where does the agent get its facts, and can you get there first.

Speaking of AI reshaping the funnel — there's a warning sign on the paid side too. Advertisers are reporting that Google's AI Overviews are quietly eating into Shopping ad clicks and impressions, because fewer people scroll past the AI summary to see the ads below it. If you're advising an ecommerce client, this is a channel-mix conversation to have now, not next quarter — Shopping budgets built on last year's click patterns might be assuming a customer journey that no longer exists.

On the retail-meets-AI front: Amazon has struck a deal with OpenAI letting its advertisers run ads inside ChatGPT. That's a genuine about-turn — Amazon spent ages blocking AI platforms from crawling its store, and now it's quietly become ChatGPT's biggest retail advertiser. With 900 million weekly users on ChatGPT, Amazon's basically conceding that conversational AI is now a real acquisition channel, not a novelty. For any client debating whether AI chat surfaces belong in the media plan — Amazon just answered that question with its wallet.

Meanwhile, if you want a reality check on what actually still drives organic visibility, there's a solid new survey out — 131 SEO experts, over a hundred ranking factors, more than thirteen thousand data points. Top three: relevance, backlinks, and content quality, with "does this actually satisfy what the person wanted" — search intent match — coming out as the single biggest lever. Original research and first-party data are climbing too, mostly because everyone's drowning in AI-generated sameness and anything genuinely proprietary stands out. Useful ammunition next time someone needs convincing that SEO investment isn't just technical box-ticking — technical SEO, per this survey, is table stakes, not a differentiator.

Zooming out a bit — Benedict Evans has a smart piece arguing AI won't automatically transform how companies work just because code got easier to write. The hard part was never the coding, it's figuring out what to automate in the first place, and most people don't naturally think that way. He points out that most employees barely touch tools like Copilot, which echoes every previous tech rollout — PCs in the eighties, browsers in the nineties. The takeaway for anyone building a business case around AI-enabled products: don't assume adoption, research it. Map the actual existing workflow before assuming people will bolt a new tool onto it.

A couple of quick ones on the paid media side. There's a detailed new LinkedIn ads playbook doing the rounds, arguing you should stop targeting by job title alone and start watching for buying signals — job postings, funding rounds, tech-stack changes — to catch prospects in an active buying window. Given B2B deals apparently need something like 88 touches across four channels over 272 days, that's a genuinely useful segmentation idea: build the ICP around timing signals, not just firmographics.

And there's a slightly cheeky but fair piece on Google Ads' interface itself — seven ways the platform's own defaults quietly nudge advertisers toward decisions that suit Google more than the client. Default dashboards favouring flattering comparisons, "optimisation scores" that can actually hurt performance, bidding settings that silently override each other. Competitor-watch note here: worth checking whether rival agencies are falling for the same defaults, because a client who dodges these traps has a real efficiency edge.

On the product-led growth side, Elena Verna's got a sharp piece cataloguing the same mistake showing up across PLG companies scaling into enterprise: they abandon the self-serve engine that built their pipeline in the first place, relabel existing revenue as "new" enterprise growth, and let sales teams dictate the roadmap. Her point, which is a good one for any business case involving segment strategy, is that self-serve and enterprise customers need fundamentally different acquisition and retention mechanics — starve one to feed the other and you eventually starve both.

Nice contrast with a genuinely fun read on brand repositioning: a piece on how legacy brands successfully "de-age" — Abercrombie, Gap, Coach, Stanley, New Balance, Chili's, even Barnes & Noble. The common thread isn't reinvention, it's recognising how your original customers have aged and evolved, then presenting the same heritage through a new cultural lens rather than chasing a younger audience that was never yours. Directly useful for journey mapping work — the question isn't "who's an 18-year-old today," it's "who is our 2005 customer now, and what do they need."

Which pairs nicely with the story of how Starface made pimple patches cool. Instead of hiding acne treatment, they made bright yellow star-shaped patches you wear on purpose, aimed squarely at Gen Z's preference for authenticity over perfection, then layered on TikTok-native content and collabs with Hello Kitty and SpongeBob. The result: customers became walking billboards with basically zero acquisition cost. Good case study if you're ever advising a client that "invisible and effective" isn't always the winning positioning — sometimes visible and fun beats it.

Quick one: IMDb has added a "Digital Creator" category to profiles, covering streamers, vloggers and influencers with no film or TV credit required. Small move, but it's IMDb quietly extending its business model to capture a customer segment — creators — it previously ignored. Competitor-watch if you're in the creator-economy space.

And a genuinely useful behavioural nugget: researchers ran eight experiments with over six thousand people and found that recommending your cheapest or mid-tier option, not your priciest, increases sales — in one case by 58%. People assume a recommendation toward the expensive option is self-serving, and they trust it less. If you can't avoid recommending the expensive item, label it "Bestseller" rather than "Recommended." Worth an actual test on a client's product page — this is a cheap, fast experiment with a real conversion upside.

Two brief ones to close out the AI-and-trust theme. Deepfakes are increasingly being used to impersonate influencers in fake sponsored ads — lifestyle influencer Emily Schuman found herself "endorsing" weight-loss drugs she'd never touched. Consumers lost an estimated $3.7 billion globally to deepfake scams this year, and platforms are struggling to enforce their own bans. And separately, there's a solid roundup of nine media pitch formats that actually get replies from journalists — surveys, proprietary data, and commentary pitches all outperform generic outreach, and a direct ask for coverage shows up in over 90% of the ones that worked. Handy if anyone on your team is prepping PR outreach.

Last one, and it's not really marketing but it'll affect any client selling into North America: Trump signed an import ban on a chunk of Canadian goods — motorcycles, whey, molasses, beer, wine, whisky — starting the 29th, and Canada's hit back with its own tariffs on twenty billion dollars of US goods. Trade war's heating up again, so if you've got clients with cross-border supply chains, that's one to flag.
