Morning, Niki. Here's your TLDR Marketing catch-up for Tuesday the 22nd, Wednesday the 23rd, and Thursday the 24th of September. Quite a lot happened, so let's get into it.

Let's start with the theme that's eating the whole industry right now: getting found inside AI answers, not just Google's ten blue links. Ahrefs launched something called Brand Radar this week, which tracks how often your brand actually gets mentioned or cited by ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity, Copilot, and Google's AI Overviews. It benchmarks you against competitors and flags content gaps, essentially telling you which buyer questions AI is answering and whether you're even in the room when it answers them. Separately, there's a workflow going around using Semrush and Claude to do "AI citation outreach" — finding third-party pages that AI models already cite and getting your brand added to them, because apparently sixty-two percent of AI citations are "ghost citations," where a page gets cited but your brand doesn't get named at all. And there's a neat little checklist doing the rounds called the perfectly optimized page for AEO — answer engine optimization — covering things like letting AI crawlers into your robots.txt and keeping a visible "last updated" date, since AI systems cite fresher pages three times more often. Put those three together and you've basically got a new competitive axis to map for any client: who owns the AI answer for your category's key buyer questions, and where are the visibility gaps you can go fill.

Speaking of AI answers, let's talk ChatGPT ads, because there were three stories this week that all point the same direction. OpenAI's ad business has apparently hit a billion dollars in annualised revenue within two hundred days of launch, and one commentator is drawing a direct line to Google's 2002 pivot to click-based ranked ads — arguing ChatGPT is now winning the exact commercial searches, running shoes, travel research, that used to be Google's bread and butter, because its answers are simply better. OpenAI even ran a webinar this week on best practice: fewer, better-funded campaigns, optimise for conversions not clicks, and pairing their Pixel with a Conversion API, which they claim lifts conversion coverage by sixty-nine percent. Worth watching if any clients are testing early. But — and this is the bit to flag before anyone gets too excited — a researcher this week also found that OpenAI is quietly tracking ChatGPT users across advertiser websites using a cookie called "obi," scraping browsing behaviour, even unencrypted location data, and filing it under "analytics" consent rather than "marketing" consent. It behaves exactly like Meta's old cross-site tracking. Given people tell ChatGPT things they'd never post on Facebook, that's a reputational landmine sitting under a genuinely promising ad platform. Good one to keep an eye on before recommending budget there.

Switching from platforms to people — influencer budgets are quietly reshuffling. Brands are now putting sixty to seventy percent of influencer spend toward mid-tier creators, the fifty-to-five-hundred-thousand follower range, because they're cheaper per view, more engaged, and frankly more trusted than the mega-creators who used to hoover up the budget. Follower count is turning into a conversation starter, not the whole pitch. For any client running influencer strategy, that's a real prompt to go do the audience research properly — map which mid-tier creators' followers actually convert, not just who's got reach, and build that into the customer journey rather than defaulting to the biggest name available.

On the subject of proof, there was a fun piece this week rounding up unconventional ways brands show off customer wins — Shopify projecting first-time merchant sales onto the Las Vegas Sphere, Stripe putting Instacart on a Times Square billboard, Pylon literally labelling logos "migrated off Zendesk." The point isn't the gimmick, it's that different proof formats land with different segments — quantified results resonate with one buyer, verifiable case studies with another. Worth testing which format actually reinforces your value prop for your specific personas rather than defaulting to a logo wall.

And staying on the theme of narrative — there's a nice piece arguing that great products still need great stories, using the Apple II as the example. It didn't take off on specs; it took off when VisiCalc gave business owners a reason to actually picture themselves using it, and that's the gap the author says AI and Tesla's self-driving marketing keep falling into — all capability, no human payoff. It's a good sense-check for positioning work: if you can't describe the tangible life-improvement in one sentence, the tech isn't the problem, the story is.

Which ties neatly into another one this week on LinkedIn content — the argument being that bad engagement is rarely bad content, it's unclear positioning. If your audience can't sum up what you deliver in one sentence, they won't stick around. It's basically the same discipline Customer Strategy applies at the org level — a quick gut-check worth running on any client's own market positioning, not just their LinkedIn feed.

Now, some genuinely useful data: Orbit Media's annual blogging survey landed, and it's a bit of a wake-up call. Only fourteen percent of marketers now say their blog delivers strong results, down from a stable twenty-to-thirty percent for over a decade. Posts have gotten shorter and faster to write, ninety-two percent of marketers now use AI to help — and none of that correlates with better performance. What actually dropped off a cliff is influencer collaboration, down from twenty-five to seven percent, plus original research and human editing. The takeaway isn't "content doesn't work anymore," it's that people stopped doing the things that worked. Good prompt to audit any client's content programme against that list before recommending more output rather than better output.

Onto retention — there's a sharp piece this week on churn. "Not enough usage" gets blamed for a quarter to forty percent of app cancellations, but it's treated as one problem when it's actually four different broken habit loops — the trigger never fires, the action never sticks, the reward goes flat, or the value just never fit the user's life in the first place. The article's strongest point: don't discount your way out of a usage problem, because the issue is perceived value, not price. For any subscription client, that's a segmentation job — pull churned cohorts apart by which loop broke, and design the fix to match, rather than reaching for a blanket win-back discount.

Two stories about the gap between what leadership wants from AI and what teams are actually doing. First, on managing AI mandates upward: sixty-nine percent of senior execs use AI less than an hour a week while telling teams to use it constantly. The fix suggested is a thirty-day audit of where AI hours actually go, then proposing a capacity budget, say ten percent of team time, with named owners and kill dates, so you're negotiating with real numbers instead of vague enthusiasm. That's basically a ready-made business-case input for anyone building an AI adoption case for a client.

Second, and related — Adverity found marketers trust human-written reports nearly twice as much as AI-generated ones, even though manual reporting has an error rate ten times higher. The real issue isn't the AI, it's that ninety-four percent of marketing departments don't even agree on their own metric definitions. So before any client invests further in AI analytics, the actual prerequisite is a boring but essential exercise: get everyone using the same definitions and business context first.

Channel-wise, worth flagging that Reddit is angling for a bigger slice of media plans — new bidding structures are already showing a fifteen percent drop in cost per acquisition, and the platform's users research and validate purchases across way more communities than the old "gamers and IT guys" stereotype suggests. If a client assumes Reddit's audience is niche, that's an assumption worth testing directly against where your actual target customers spend time.

On the more traditional side, Lob's direct mail report is a nice reminder that not everything's gone digital — eighty-seven percent of people read mail the same day it arrives, and trust in direct mail is actually climbing. But the personalisation story splits sharply by generation: Boomers want practical offers, younger audiences want life-stage relevance. Good segmentation data if a client's channel mix still includes physical mail.

Meanwhile, at the macro level, there's a piece contrasting AI-automated ad platforms — Google's Performance Max, Meta's Advantage+ — which are growing fast and expected to be over a quarter of all ad revenue by 2030, against TV, which is growing slower than inflation despite billions poured into sports rights. The warning attached is a good one for any client eyeing automation: feed it weak first-party data and you just get bad decisions made faster. And on a similar note, AppLovin says its platform ad spend is up sixty percent year-on-year to roughly twenty billion dollars, with its CEO making the case that personalised in-game ads are an underrated opportunity bigger than the hype around language models. Competitor-watch territory if you've got clients in mobile or gaming.

On the agentic commerce front, two stories worth pairing. Meta's new AI agent Muse is supposed to make phone calls on your behalf — book a table, sort a query — except it turns out a chunk of those calls are secretly being routed to human workers in call centres, and testers weren't told a human was on the line until afterwards. Meanwhile a Visa survey found only eleven percent of small merchants are actually ready for AI shopping agents to transact on their behalf — most don't have the structured product data or the ability to tell agent traffic from human traffic. Different ends of the same problem: the tech is racing ahead of trust, consent, and basic infrastructure readiness. Anyone advising on agentic commerce should be running an honest readiness audit before promising clients revenue from it.

Quick one on research methods worth knowing about: synthetic surveys, AI systems that simulate actual behaviour rather than asking people what they'd do, are gaining serious traction — EY found real advisor retention matched AI simulations far better than traditional surveys, which overstated loyalty by double. If you're validating a business case on customer intent data, it's worth remembering traditional surveys have a habit of flattering the answer people think sounds good.

And a nice one on brand building — there's a piece distinguishing "branded objects," generic stuff with a logo slapped on, from "brand objects," things so distinctive they could only be that brand, the LEGO brick, the IKEA FRAKTA bag. The argument is that these survive in people's lives long after the ad spend stops. Worth remembering next time a client starts with "which channel" instead of "what would people actually want to keep."

A couple of quick regulatory notes to file away. Australia's under-16 social media ban has deactivated millions of accounts, but over eighty percent of kids are just using the same platforms anyway, or migrating to Roblox, WhatsApp, and regional apps most brands aren't tracking yet — a reminder that bans redirect audiences, they rarely remove them. And California just signed a law fining creators five thousand dollars a post for undisclosed political payments, closing a loophole a lot of campaigns were quietly using.

And last couple of quick hits: OpenAI dropped two new cheaper models, Sol and Luna, half the price of the last generation and reportedly beating Claude Opus on several benchmarks — mostly a cost story for anyone building on their API. And there's a fun little case study of a six-figure Substack business running its entire growth function through named AI agents, each owning one channel, with the founder keeping final send approval on everything — a decent template if you ever need to show a client what disciplined, targets-first AI adoption looks like in practice, rather than the vague "let's use AI more" version.
