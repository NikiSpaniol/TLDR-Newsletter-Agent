First up, Meta's been busy. They've just dropped Muse Spark Open, and it's a genuinely wild spec sheet — an 816-billion-parameter open-weight model, though only 42 billion of those parameters are active at any one time, and it's packing a context window of a full million tokens. Translation: it can hold an enormous amount of information in its head at once, which makes it well suited to coding and multi-step "agentic" tasks — the kind where the model has to plan, act, check its work, and keep going.

Here's the bit that's actually turning heads though: Meta got this running on consumer hardware. Specifically, two Apple M5 Ultra Mac Studios wired together using Apple's MLX framework, using the chip's unified memory to effectively behave like one supercomputer with a terabyte of memory. No data-centre GPU farm required. They showed it doing autonomous iOS development and video editing entirely on-device, which is a pretty strong flex. It's live now on Hugging Face, Google Cloud, AWS SageMaker, and through inference partners like Together AI and Groq, with a commercial license that's free for most developers. Meta's also saying it clears their internal safety thresholds for responsible deployment, so this isn't just a research toy — it's meant to be used.

Nothing here that changes a customer strategy right now, but it's worth filing away as a sign of where the ground is shifting: frontier-grade AI capability is quietly becoming something that runs on a desk, not just in a hyperscaler's data centre. That has knock-on implications for any client thinking about build-versus-buy on AI infrastructure — worth keeping on the radar for when that conversation comes up.

And Microsoft isn't sitting still either. They've announced they've finished pre-training their next frontier model, called MAI-Base-2. It's a mixture-of-experts model — meaning instead of one giant network doing everything, it's built from specialised sub-networks that get switched in depending on the task — with 80 billion active parameters out of a total of 2.8 trillion. Microsoft's headline claim is that it outperforms other open-weight models while being four to twelve times more efficient to train than DeepSeek's V4 Pro Base, which has been one of the benchmarks to beat this year.

Not a lot of detail has been made public beyond that yet, so take the efficiency numbers as Microsoft's own framing for now rather than independently verified. But strategically, it's another data point in the same story as the Meta release: the two biggest platform players are both racing to prove they can do more with less compute, which is ultimately a cost argument as much as a capability one. Worth a line in any competitor-watch deck tracking how the AI infrastructure players are positioning on efficiency versus raw scale.
