Let's start with the awkward one. OpenAI has fired three safety researchers for allegedly sharing confidential company information with an outside AI safety organisation. The timing is not great for them — this lands just two days after the New York Times reported that OpenAI executives had been waving off internal warnings about safety practices in favour of shipping faster. It's also not a first-time thing; they let go of two researchers on similar grounds back in 2024. Nothing actionable for us here, just one to file under "watch the trust narrative" if you're ever benchmarking OpenAI's brand risk against competitors.

Now, onto something genuinely useful. There's a new category of AI model quietly forming, and four different companies dropped entries in it over our two days — Cloudflare, Amazon, a solo developer named Jared Palmer, and Perplexity. They're called decision models, and the pitch is simple: instead of a chatbot that writes you an essay, these models just pick an option from a list and give you a confidence score, in milliseconds, on a laptop. Cloudflare's version, called Clef, is topping the leaderboards and comes with a reinforcement learning fine-tuning service attached. Amazon's entry, Strands Decider, is a lean two-billion-parameter model built for things like routing a task to the right tool or catching an agent before it does something dumb. Perplexity's take, called pplx-decider, is the one worth sitting with for a second — they trained it specifically to route customer requests to the right team, billing versus technical support versus sales, and pushed accuracy up from about seventy-five percent to nearly eighty-six percent compared to the base model. That's a very direct customer-strategy story: this is the triage layer sitting in front of your contact centre, deciding who gets a human and how fast. If you're building a case for AI in customer service, decision models like this are the cheaper, faster, more auditable alternative to routing everything through a big general chatbot — worth adding as its own line item in any cost model, separate from the flashy generative stuff, because the economics and the risk profile are genuinely different. And competitively, it's worth mapping who's offering what here, because this is shaping up to be a quiet land grab underneath all the agent hype.

Speaking of agents — Meta's Muse had a big few days. The strategically interesting piece is about how Meta plans to make money from it. Rivals like DoorDash and Airbnb are building their own shopping agents, but those agents have a structural problem: they can never honestly tell you to buy from a competitor, because they're built by the seller. Muse can. Meta's bet is that Muse stays genuinely neutral and ad-free inside the app, and instead makes its money on the back end — a cut of transactions, like a payment network, plus sharper ad targeting on Facebook and Instagram once it sees what you're shopping for. That's a real moat: owning the surfaces where the ad dollars actually land means Meta doesn't need to monetise the agent directly at all. If you're ever sizing up a client's agent strategy, this is the question to ask first — are they trying to make money inside the agent, or are they using the agent to feed a business they already own? That's a very different business case depending on the answer. Meta's been moving fast on the ecosystem side too — they've open-sourced the code so hobbyists can build their own Muse gadgets on things like Raspberry Pis, and they're giving away five thousand of their own home hub devices. And there's a quietly big structural shift underneath all of this: these personal agents are turning out to be much easier to deploy at scale than people expected, because armies of AI agents don't have the turf wars and ego problems humans do — OpenAI reportedly had thousands of agents self-coordinate to crack a famously hard maths problem with barely any human direction. The flip side is that someone now has to prove a real human is behind each agent's requests, which is why identity tools like World ID are getting bolted on after Amazon straight-up blocked Muse for not disclosing it was a bot.

Quick one on talent: Anthropic is putting a hundred million dollars into something called the Claude Frontier Academy, aiming to train ten thousand engineers by the end of 2027 to properly embed Claude into big companies — Accenture, Morgan Stanley, Novo Nordisk are already in. It's structured like a medical residency, with certification at the end. Given Anthropic is heading toward a mooted two-trillion-dollar IPO valuation while still losing over eight billion a year, this reads as them building the demand-side infrastructure to justify that number — worth remembering next time a client cites a vendor's revenue growth without asking how much of it is subsidised adoption.

On the sovereignty front, two stories rhymed nicely. Aleph Alpha released Kolibri, a big open-weight German-English model built specifically so European governments and regulated industries can run it entirely on their own servers, no data ever leaving the building. And separately, there's a sharp piece of thinking out of Australia arguing that real AI sovereignty isn't about building your own national chatbot — it's about being able to switch providers, audit a system, and walk away if you need to. Their practical advice: for most institutional work, document classification, internal support, regulatory search, use the smallest model that can safely do the job, hosted locally, rather than defaulting to the biggest frontier model available. That's a genuinely good lens for any public-sector business case — the sovereignty question isn't "whose model," it's "can we leave."

There was also a sobering numbers exercise worth knowing about: someone did the maths on how many AI agents the chip industry can actually support through 2027, and even at a conservative slice of projected capacity, running them all would cost two-and-a-half to five trillion dollars a year — against roughly one trillion in projected revenue for the whole sector. In other words, there's a real possibility the industry is building more agent capacity than anyone currently has a use for. Good context to have in your back pocket next time a client's business case assumes AI usage will simply keep climbing in a straight line.

A couple of research culture pieces are worth a mention too, even without a direct client hook. A Harvard physicist spent three months pairing with Claude and produced thirty-six scientific papers across eighteen fields, by figuring out which problems actually suit what the model is good at — lots of maths and pattern-spotting, with a human expert always needed to turn a technically correct answer into something actually meaningful. And on the flip side, there's a smart warning doing the rounds that AI is quietly making research less collaborative — the same way a driverless car removes the mildly annoying small talk with a taxi driver, chatting with an LLM instead of a messy, credit-sharing human collaborator feels like pure convenience, but you lose the friction that used to spark the best ideas. Worth remembering that logic applies just as much inside a strategy team as it does in a lab.
