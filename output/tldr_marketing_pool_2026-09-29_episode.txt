Morning, Niki. Let's get you caught up on marketing from Friday the twenty-fifth and Monday the twenty-eighth of September. There's a real thread running through this one: AI is quietly rewriting how customers search, decide, and buy — and almost every story today is really about that in disguise.

Let's start with search, because the ground is shifting fast there. There's a workshop coming up from Marcel Santilli, CEO of GrowthX AI, and his central argument is worth stealing even if you never register. He says everyone's obsessing over "AI visibility scores" — tracking how often a brand shows up in a chatbot answer — and that's the wrong metric entirely. What actually matters is mapping the real questions buyers are asking in your market, then figuring out where you answer those questions well, where a competitor answers them better, and where nobody's answering them at all. That's not an SEO tactic, that's a competitive positioning exercise. If you're building out a competitor map for a client, this gives you a genuinely new axis to plot on it: not just price or features, but who "wins" the buyer's question in AI search.

That theme comes back hard in a second piece on winning AI search through creators. The headline number: ninety-four percent of marketers say AI research is now part of the customer journey, but eighty-six percent have no system for managing how AI actually describes their brand. And here's the kicker — less than ten percent of what AI says about a brand comes from content the brand actually owns. It's creators, forums, and earned media doing the talking. So the practical move for any customer journey map right now is to add a new stage: not just "considers alternatives" but "asks an AI, which pulls from sources you don't control." Worth flagging in any journey workshop this quarter.

And then there's a slightly mischievous data point out of the ChatGPT ad world. Marketers running ads inside ChatGPT are seeing their organic traffic jump when the ads are live, and drop when they pause — one advertiser saw a fivefold lift. Sounds like OpenAI might be quietly favoring advertisers the way Google's long been accused of. The more boring, more likely explanation: it's a measurement error. Paid clicks aren't properly tagged in Google Analytics, so they're getting dumped into the same "organic" bucket as genuine unpaid mentions, making the lift look real when it might just be an accounting illusion. Why this matters for you: if a client asks whether they should defensively spend on ChatGPT ads just in case it helps their organic standing, that's exactly the kind of business-case assumption you want to pressure-test before it becomes a line item nobody can justify or kill.

Shifting from search to shopping — there's a great one here on why AI-assisted buying still ends at Amazon. Sixty-one million Americans now use AI to research a purchase, but sixty percent of them still check out on Amazon anyway. And this is the fascinating bit: in a controlled test, even when people were told an AI could source the exact same product, same price, same shipping, same returns policy, from three different retailers — forty percent still picked Amazon. It's not about convenience anymore. It's trust that if something goes wrong, Amazon will fix it. The article compares it to Febreze: the product worked fine, it just needed reframing around what people were really buying, which was confidence, not odor removal. For anyone doing customer research on agentic commerce right now, this is a big one — the purchase driver is quietly shifting from "how easy is checkout" to "who protects me if this goes wrong." That's a business case worth revisiting if you're advising a retailer on agent-readiness.

Speaking of rebuilding around AI agents from the inside — McDonald's has a big structural play underway. They're piloting a media network across four hundred and fifty of their own restaurants, selling ad space on kiosks, apps, and menu boards, aiming to make it a billion-dollar business. Alongside that, they're consolidating everything — ordering, loyalty, marketing, data — into one unified, AI-ready app platform rolling out globally by twenty twenty-eight. The bit that matters for us: none of this works without granular customer journey mapping across their own channels, figuring out exactly which touchpoints and moments are valuable enough for a third-party brand to pay for. It's a clean real-world example of "know your journey well enough to monetize it," which is a great case study if you're ever building the business case for a client's own owned-media ambitions.

Also rebuilding from the inside out — marketing data platforms themselves. There's a solid piece on what's being called the Agentic CDP, essentially embedding AI agents directly inside the governed data platform rather than bolting them on as middleware. The stat that jumps out: seventy-four percent of US consumers now use AI tools somewhere in their shopping journey, but only ten percent of companies have gotten AI agents working properly outside of IT. The strategic read here is simple — customer engagement workflows built for humans browsing at human speed don't hold up when agents are transacting on someone's behalf in seconds. Worth flagging in any journey redesign work: are we still mapping for browsers, or are we now mapping for agents acting on browsers' behalf?

On the sales side, two pieces worth pairing. SaaStr AI shared how they replaced their creaky old contact form with a dual AI-agent system — one qualifying inbound leads live on their sponsorship page, another handling self-serve prospectuses — and it's driven a sixty percent jump in new business with just three humans running it. But the real insight isn't the tech stack, it's that this only works because their buyer is tech-native and comfortable being qualified by a bot. Compare that to the second piece, which argues the opposite is true elsewhere: as GTM gets more automated, genuinely researched, personalized human outreach is becoming the differentiator, precisely because so much of what buyers now receive is generic AI-generated sludge. Put those two together and you get a genuinely useful client exercise — map your buyer profile first, tech-native versus traditional, then decide whether your channel architecture should lean into automation or lean into visible human effort, because the wrong choice for the wrong buyer just reads as friction.

Zooming out a level, there's a good piece on whether marketing teams are shrinking. Short answer: not headcount, but shape. Thirty-nine percent of CMOs are planning cuts, yet the fastest-growing B2B companies are actively hiring marketers, and every company that's raised over half a billion dollars has open marketing roles. The model that's winning replaces layered specialist teams with small outcome-owning pods of AI-empowered generalists, plus a thinner layer of deep experts doing the high-judgment work AI can't touch — positioning, big launches, taste. If you're advising on org design or talent strategy anytime soon, that's the shape to have in your back pocket.

Two more worth a mention before we get to the fun stuff. First, a nice practical one on customer language — the plain finding is that most companies describe their product in jargon that doesn't match how customers actually talk about their problem, and that mismatch is now costing more than ever, hitting conversion, ad costs, and even AI visibility. The fix is refreshingly unglamorous: pull real phrases from reviews, support tickets, and sales calls, and rewrite your headlines and calls to action in that language every quarter. Simple, cheap, and a good one to build into any content or positioning workstream.

Second, RevenueCat crunched a year of data across seventeen thousand apps on free trial length, and the pattern's nuanced — longer trials generally lift renewal, but conversion peaks around ten to sixteen days for monthly plans before tailing off, while annual plans keep improving out to a month. AI apps, interestingly, see diminishing returns past that same ten-to-sixteen-day mark because they're expensive to run per trial day. If you're building a business case around trial length for a subscription client, don't just assume "longer is better" — test it against category and time-to-value, because the data says it's genuinely not one-size-fits-all.

One more worth flagging on the human side of all this AI: a Gallup survey run with Microsoft found seventy-four percent of Americans worry about AI's implications, even among people using it daily — the highest anxiety of any Western country surveyed. Compare that to Singapore, where usage is lower but trust and optimism are much higher. The takeaway for anyone doing customer messaging work: usage doesn't equal comfort. If you're segmenting an audience around AI-powered products, "they use it" and "they trust it" are two completely different customer groups, and your messaging needs to know which one it's talking to.
