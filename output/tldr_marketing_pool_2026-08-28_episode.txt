Let's start with some genuinely good news: US ecommerce growth is back in double digits. Q2 came in at 12.2% year-over-year, following 10.1% in Q1 — the first back-to-back double-digit quarters since before the pandemic, pushing ecommerce's share of total retail up to 17.1%. But don't get too excited before reading the fine print. General merchandise and sporting goods posted the flashiest growth rates, over 20% each, while clothing and accessories crawled along at just 3.8% — and yet clothing still brought in more raw dollars. Growth rate and actual dollar contribution are two very different stories. If you're building a business case around this rebound, the real work is pulling apart your own category and segment data rather than riding the headline number, because whether this justifies more investment in fulfilment or acquisition depends entirely on which shelf you're sitting on.

Next, a genuinely useful one if you've got twenty minutes on the train: is the era of the fifteen-thousand-tool martech landscape finally ending? Short answer, yes, sort of. Organisations are waking up to the fact that more apps don't equal better marketing — you just get fragmented data, integration headaches, and hidden costs nobody budgeted for. AI is speeding up the consolidation because a single platform can now do content, analytics, personalisation and campaign orchestration that used to need four separate logins. But it's not a wholesale cull — the winning pattern is what the article calls "selective consolidation": unify the core, keep a few specialist tools, stitch it together with open APIs. For anyone advising on this, the starting point isn't the vendor pitch deck, it's the customer journey — map how people actually move across channels first, then decide what gets centralised and what earns the right to stay standalone.

Sticking with the theme of "how long should you actually wait before judging something" — twenty-eight creator economy experts were asked how long brands should test an influencer partnership before pulling the plug. Most brands currently give it one to two months. The expert consensus is three to six. Why the gap? Attribution systems undercount influencer-driven conversions by 40 to 60%, YouTube partnerships that actually work average over a year, and most paid social conversions need three or more touchpoints before they convert at all. The upshot: 63% of brand-creator relationships are one-and-done, but it's the 37% that get extended that deliver almost all the results. If you're designing a test-and-learn framework for a client's creator strategy, the real question isn't post volume, it's matching the evaluation window to that category's actual sales cycle — so you're not cutting a partnership right as it was about to pay off.
