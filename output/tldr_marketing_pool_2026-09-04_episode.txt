First up, a quieter but important one: music licensing claims are becoming a real headache for marketing teams. Rights holders are increasingly going after brands for unlicensed music in social posts, ad campaigns, and influencer content — and a single registered work can trigger statutory damages of up to $150,000 per infringement, no proof of actual loss required. The kicker is that insurers often deny these claims outright if willful infringement is alleged, so the safety net you think you have might not be there. This is being driven by content creation spreading way beyond agencies, old undocumented content still sitting online, and AI-generated content that might be echoing existing works without anyone noticing. If you've got clients running influencer programs, this is worth a quiet audit: do they have sync and master use rights documented, are influencer agreements actually requiring indemnification, and does their insurance policy reflect how content actually gets made in 2026. Most of these disputes are entirely preventable — which also means most exposure is entirely fixable, if someone bothers to look.

Sticking with search and content for a second — there's a nice bit of data on what actually makes a listicle rank well. Freshness is the biggest lever: pages with recent dates are far more likely to land in the top three. Counted listicles beat editorial roundups — "17 Best Tools" consistently outperforms a vague roundup — and bigger numbers in the title do better too. Worth flagging if any client is leaning on listicle content for AI visibility: fresh dates, explicit counts, and don't over-engineer the structure with a wall of headings.

Speaking of AI visibility, OpenAI is trying to make ChatGPT advertising stupidly easy — you paste in your website URL, and it auto-generates ad creative, copy, and even suggests customer journeys, all in under sixty seconds. They're sweetening it with $500 in matching ad credit, classic playbook. But the strategic point is bigger than the tool itself: OpenAI is explicitly pitching ChatGPT as a single consolidated place where discovery and purchase intent happen, instead of the usual fragmented multi-channel scramble. If that pitch has any legs, it's worth revisiting where in the funnel your clients assume they're winning customers — because if a chunk of research and intent-forming is quietly moving into a chat window, your channel mix and attribution model need to know that before the budget does.

Quick one while we're on Google: the Shopping tab now lets mobile users filter results by trusted review source — YouTube, Reddit, and so on — under a "Top Reviewed" filter. Handy for shoppers, and a small signal that review provenance is becoming a first-class sorting mechanic, not just a nice-to-have.

Now, a genuinely fun one. Remember Vine? Its spiritual successor, Divine, launched publicly and its whole pitch is friction. No AI-generated content, no uploads, no ads — everything has to be shot live in-app and is cryptographically verified as real. The recommendation algorithm is even deliberately detuned so creators and audiences build repeat relationships instead of endless stranger-scrolling. The founder's bet is that people are so fatigued by AI slop everywhere else that they'll actually pay a premium for verified human content. Whether that's a real market or a nice story is genuinely an open question — and that's the useful bit for you. This is a clean case for customer research before strategy: is there a segment that will actually trade convenience for authenticity assurance, or is this a lovely idea that a tiny niche cares about? Worth mapping as a competitor axis if any client sits in the creator or social space — authenticity-as-differentiator versus reach-as-differentiator.

That theme comes up again with Charli XCX, of all people, who's been publicly pushing what she calls "anti-marketing" — deliberately small, one-to-one communication instead of chasing scale. The broader argument is that influence is migrating to opt-in spaces like Substack, Discord, and Slack, where small communities are deeply invested rather than passively scrolling, and even LinkedIn's algorithm is now rewarding individual voices over brand accounts. The strategic read here: trust is starting to beat reach as the thing that actually drives marketing effectiveness. If you're building a customer journey map for a client, it's worth explicitly separating touchpoints where broadcast reach still works from touchpoints where a smaller, deeper relationship channel would actually convert better — because the metrics that matter are genuinely different in each.

Google's also been quietly reshaping how people search. AI Overviews are now auto-expanding to full length on certain queries, no more clicking "show more," and shoving traditional organic results further down the page — with the follow-up question box already open, nudging people deeper into AI mode rather than out to a website. Publishers hate it, understandably, since it's more traffic diverted before anyone clicks through. Worth factoring into any content or SEO-dependent customer acquisition plan: the assumption that a good ranking equals a click is getting shakier by the month.

Bigger news: the FTC sued Amazon on the 31st, backed by twenty-two state attorneys general, alleging Amazon quietly built undisclosed "soft reserve prices" into its ad auctions back in 2019 and pocketed more than $20 billion in hidden surcharges from advertisers who had no idea. Amazon's pushing back hard, saying its systems have actually saved advertisers $8 billion since 2021 and that the FTC has no evidence of real consumer harm. This lands right after last year's $2.5 billion Prime settlement and ahead of an antitrust trial — so Amazon's regulatory pile is getting tall. Nothing to action here, just worth keeping on the radar given how central Amazon's ad platform is to a lot of clients' media mix.

Now here's one I think you'll actually enjoy — a really concrete customer research example. Someone built a pre-Black-Friday plan for a $470 LED skincare mask by actually studying the audience first, rather than just booking a November promo. Turns out this buyer group under-indexes on every social platform compared to average users, but massively over-indexes on Claude usage — sixteen point nine percent versus a five percent national average. They're deep in consideration mode, comparing efficacy claims and hunting for dermatologist endorsements months before they buy. So instead of a Black Friday blitz, the recommended play is a three-month runway: authoritative answer-content through August and September, borrowing credibility from trusted beauty publications through October, then comparison content right before the sale actually lands. It's a lovely, simple reminder for any business case you're building — go find out where the customer is actually making the decision, because it's very often not in the week you're planning to sell to them.

Related and slightly less cheerful — the SEO agencies that built their whole retainer model around comparison and roundup pages just had a rough month. ChatGPT halved its citations of listicle content after an August update, and Google's spam update a few days later specifically targeted that same scaled comparison-content playbook. The pages that existed purely to catch AI queries had no real audience behind them, so when the retrieval rules shifted, they just evaporated. The lesson for anyone advising on content strategy: cheap, volume-based content built to game a specific algorithm is now a fragile asset, not a durable one.

On the retail side, there's a sharp piece arguing the story isn't "stores are dying" — it's that the traditional merchant model is dying. Buying inventory months in advance and betting on demand forecasts is a brutal model when consumer preferences swing fast and social media can create or kill demand overnight. The fix gaining traction, especially in Europe and Asia and increasingly at places like Target and Macy's, is a store-in-store model — brands own their own inventory and pricing inside retailer-owned space, so the retailer just monetizes the traffic and curation instead of carrying all the risk. If you're building a retail business case, this is a genuinely useful lens: map out who in the value chain is actually holding the demand-forecasting risk, because that's shifting, and whoever's still holding it the old-fashioned way is the one most exposed.

Quick, slightly nerdy one — researchers tracked 1.8 million AI prompts and found that simply showing people what their usage is costing them changes behaviour: they write more deliberate prompts, waste less time generating throwaway variants, and end up happier with the output. If you're advising anyone building or buying AI tools for their teams, this is a genuine pricing and UX insight — visible usage meters aren't just a billing feature, they're a behaviour design lever.

Affiliate marketing's getting a rethink too, and it's a good one for journey mapping. The old model was affiliate sitting right at the bottom of the funnel, catching people who were already sold. But now that so much discovery happens through AI tools, review sites, and creators before anyone hits a brand's website, affiliate partners are increasingly shaping which brands even make the shortlist. The practical implication: broaden partner selection beyond coupon and cashback sites to editorial and creator partners, judge them on trust and influence rather than raw traffic, and stop measuring everything on last-click — because that undersells everyone who influenced the decision earlier in the journey.

There's also a broader cultural thread running through several campaigns right now — brands like Etsy, TD Bank, Polaroid, and Pinterest are leaning hard into "humanity" as a message, using handmade aesthetics, real film, behind-the-scenes footage, all as a counterpoint to rising public anxiety about AI. Even Liquid Death got in on it with a tongue-in-cheek "saving humanity" gag. The read for positioning work: if a client's audience is feeling anxious or out of control because of AI creeping into their world, human-centred messaging can be a real differentiator — but only with genuine substance behind it, because "humanity" is rapidly becoming the new empty buzzword if brands don't back it up.

On the PR side, there's a nice practical piece from Becca Peel at Tank on why journalists now demand real human case studies rather than just data — newsrooms are thinner and need exclusive, verifiable, people-driven angles that a Google search can't already answer. Her advice: source through personal networks and cold outreach, verify people properly before pitching them, and aim to have a case study ready for eight out of ten campaign pitches. For anyone doing customer research, it's a good nudge to actively be on the lookout for customers with genuinely differentiated stories, not just satisfied ones.
