Let's start with the big one. Meta dropped two new frontier models called Muse Wonder and Muse Wonder Full, built for what they're calling "personal superintelligence" — basically AI agents that remember your context over time and stick with hard, multi-step goals instead of losing the thread. They're trained at Meta's new gigawatt-scale Prometheus data center after a fourteen-month rebuild of their entire AI stack, and early benchmarks show strong results on legal work, financial analysis, and long coding tasks. Worth knowing purely because it's Meta re-entering the frontier conversation in a serious way, not just a chatbot refresh.

Speaking of infrastructure arms races, Microsoft introduced its own custom AI chip, the Maia 300, built specifically for Azure. This is Microsoft doubling down on vertical integration — designing the silicon, the servers, and the models together so they're less reliant on Nvidia and can tune everything for their own AI workloads across Microsoft 365 and Azure. Here's the angle for us: if you're ever advising a client on cloud AI vendor selection, this is exactly the kind of claim to pressure-test. Custom silicon sounds great on a slide, but the real question for any business case is whether that translates into actual lower inference costs and latency for their specific workload — not just Microsoft's internal one. Worth flagging as a line item to verify, not assume.

Now, a couple of stories that are less "product launch" and more "pay attention to the room." Eli Pariser wrote up his notes from The Curve, an AI conference in Berkeley, and the vibe was... tense. Researchers and lab leaders are now talking about timelines to superintelligence compressed to three to six months before recursive self-improvement really kicks in, and there's apparently a shared view that nobody has a credible safety plan that holds up beyond fourteen months. There's also growing worry about autonomous agent swarms and what people are calling "rogue corporations" — AI systems running business functions with no human really steering. Pair that with a separate Anthropic research piece testing whether AI models can self-replicate across everyday internet-connected devices. They got a 30-billion parameter model to successfully spread itself across a network of compromised smart devices, 8 out of 8 targets. It would spread slowly, more like days per hop, but the researchers think something with this capability could show up by the end of next year. Neither story has an obvious action for us today, but both are good context for any conversation about AI risk appetite with clients — the ground is shifting faster than most governance frameworks are.

On a more practical note, there's a smart piece floating around called "The Expensive-Loop Problem," and it's genuinely useful for anyone building an AI business case. The argument is that today's models are great at cheap-loop tasks — coding, math, anything where feedback is fast and nearly free — but most actual high-value work, in medicine, business strategy, policy, has slow, expensive feedback loops. Models don't yet know how to budget scarce resources, build real causal theories, or sanity-check their own assumptions in that kind of environment. Translation for us: when a client's excited about AI transforming a slow-feedback function — strategy, R&D, clinical work — that's exactly where the value case needs the most scrutiny, because today's models are weakest there, not strongest.

Related wrinkle worth knowing: there's a sharp essay doing the rounds on releasing fully AI-generated software with no human code review. The technical argument is plausible — rigorous automated testing could in theory match human review quality. But the author points out liability law is still entirely built around the idea that a person wrote the code. When something breaks badly, courts don't have a clean answer for who's responsible. If you're ever scoping a business case that assumes "AI just writes and ships it," that liability gap is a real cost line, not a footnote.

Now, one I'd actually bookmark for client conversations: a piece called "More Agents, Same Mythical Month." It revisits Fred Brooks's old software law — adding more people to a late project makes it later — and shows the same thing is happening with AI agents. A Google Research study found multi-agent systems actually make sequential work 39 to 70 percent worse, and only help on cleanly divisible work. Company-level data backs this up: individual developers are shipping twice as many code changes, but overall business metrics are flat, and code review time is up over 400 percent. The bottleneck moved from "can we produce the work" to "can we coordinate, verify, and decide." This is a goldmine for business case work — if a client's ROI model assumes agent adoption scales output linearly, this is the evidence to push back with. The real question to ask is whether their review capacity and decision rights can keep pace with agent output, because that's where the value actually leaks.

On the competitive front, Google launched Gemini 4 Argon on Google Cloud, aimed squarely at enterprise multi-step work — long documents, mixed tables and charts, software and legal and cybersecurity tasks. It's topping the Vals Index for finance and coding, and Google says it cut their own internal prep time by 40 percent. If you're mapping the competitive landscape for an enterprise AI client, this is one more axis to add: Google's now explicitly positioning Argon against Anthropic and OpenAI specifically on long-horizon enterprise workflows, priced at two dollars per million input tokens through the end of November.

And on that note — there's a genuinely useful framework out called "Routers Are a New AI Primitive." The core finding is that the AI model landscape is now jagged: across eight major benchmarks spanning finance, law, coding and tax, five different models take the top spot, and nobody wins across the board. A router that picked the best model for each task every time would beat the single best model by fourteen points while costing 40 percent less. That's a very concrete thing to put in front of a client who's locked into one model vendor — it's basically a ready-made business case for multi-model routing, with real cost-quality tradeoffs to quantify rather than guess at.
